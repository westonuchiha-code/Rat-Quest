# Borrow Ocarina's choreography, keep RatQuest's jump

Ocarina of Time's combat was not built to be deep. It was built to be **staged like a sword fight in a samurai drama**: Z-targeting picks one opponent, the others hold back, the hero circles that one on an invisible tether, and enemies like the Stalfos change what they do depending on whether they are the one locked on. Its dungeons were drawn like houses, with rooms sized to Link's body, and their designer ramped them from one puzzle room to whole puzzle dungeons to "everything you've learned" plus platforming at the end. Its movement dropped the jump button so the game could choose Link's leaps for him, show off dives and drops, and keep the action simple, a choice Miyamoto made against the wishes of a team that had just made Mario 64. The parallels with RatQuest are real and close: the game already has a Zelda-style lock-on, a director that makes enemies take turns (Koizumi's exact idea, arrived at separately), a hub of doors that is almost exactly Miyamoto's first plan for Ocarina, and gear that opens old places. But RatQuest wants a harder fight and a freer jump than Ocarina chose, so the useful lessons are about **structure, staging and level craft**, not about difficulty or movement feel. The strongest specific lessons for this game: make the lock-on matter to the enemies, fix the move set's numbers before more levels are rebuilt (the Water Temple shows what happens otherwise), fill empty space with routes rather than pickups, and make every reward useful where it is found. Each suggestion in the last section says what it rests on and how well that is sourced.

## How sure each finding is, and what could not be read

The notes behind this report were gathered in two passes. The first could open no site; the second read pages in full (`research_notes/README.md` lists what was read and what was not, for all three research projects). In this report a finding is called **strong** when it comes from a developer interview read in full or from the game's own code, **fair** when it comes from a secondary page read in full (Wikipedia, critics' articles, speedrun measurements), and **weak** when it was seen only as a search-tool summary of a page nobody here read.

Three kinds of primary source were read. The **Iwata Asks** interviews (Nintendo's own, 2011, made for the 3DS remake) are the largest; they are English translations of people remembering events from the mid-1990s, so they are hindsight. The **1101 interviews** (1998, translated by glitterberri.com) and a **1999 Miyamoto strategy-guide interview** (translated by shmuplations.com) are nearer the time. The game's **decompilation** (fans have turned the cartridge's machine code back into readable source and published it on GitHub as zeldaret/oot) gives exact numbers for one enemy, the Stalfos, and for Link's movement and falls. One note on names: the 1101 translation credits the dungeon designer as "Eiji Onozuka", while later sources credit Eiji Aonuma. They are the same person: Wikipedia's page on him says he worked on Ocarina as Onozuka and that his surname changed to Aonuma afterwards ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eiji_Aonuma)). Where this report says "the dungeon designer", that is him.

What could not be read: zeldawiki.wiki, zeldadungeon.net (including its copy of a 1997 Famimaga 64 interview on auto-jump), iwataasks.nintendo.com (the Nintendo UK mirror was used instead), goombastomp.com, the Boss Keys video series on Ocarina (amara.org and YouTube captions), Aonuma's GDC 2004 talk transcript, any Game Maker's Toolkit, Edge, Eurogamer or Polygon piece, and Steve Swink's Game Feel. The ZeldaSpeedRuns attacks page returned "not found". The critpoints.net, ScreenRant and Nintendo World Report pages were seen only as search summaries. One Iwata Asks page ("The Legend of Zelda with Chanbara-style Action") failed for the combat researcher but was read in full by the movement researcher, so its quotes stand. Large gaps remain: the exact controls of sidestepping and backflipping, most enemies' tells, how each boss tests its dungeon's item, how items and songs gate the world, and the context-sensitive button's design history have no sourced account here.

Evidence about the **2026 Switch 2 remake** is kept apart throughout. It describes what Nintendo changed 28 years later, not what the 1998 team intended.

## Z-targeting was stage-fight choreography turned into a rule

The lock-on exists because the team refused to hide Link. Koizumi, who modelled him, "couldn't stand to see my Link not appear," and a first-person prototype was dropped as "not interesting visually"; keeping Link on screen made "how to handle the camera and battles" really hard, and "in order to solve those problems, we had to create a bunch of new devices, one of which was Z Targeting" ([Iwata Asks Vol.2 p.3](https://www.nintendo.com/en-gb/Iwata-Asks/Iwata-Asks-The-Legend-of-Zelda-Ocarina-of-Time-3D/Vol-2-Original-Development-Staff-Part-1/3-Let-s-Go-to-Toei-Kyoto-Studio-Park-/3-Let-s-Go-to-Toei-Kyoto-Studio-Park--231680.html)). The problem had already bitten them in Super Mario 64, where, Osawa says, trying to read a sign "sometimes you would just go around it in circles" because the axes did not line up (same page).

The answer came from a trip to Toei Kyoto Studio Park, a theme park of samurai film sets. Koizumi watched a hero surrounded by a gang and saw the trick: "**The enemies don't all attack at once. First, one attacks while the others wait. When the first guy goes down, the next one steps in.**" Without that, "the enemies would swarm the player all at once, so it would be a mess," so "Z Targeting flags one particular opponent, telling the other enemies to wait... the moment you beat that one, you can switch the Z Targeting to the next opponent" ([Iwata Asks Vol.2 p.3](https://www.nintendo.com/en-gb/Iwata-Asks/Iwata-Asks-The-Legend-of-Zelda-Ocarina-of-Time-3D/Vol-2-Original-Development-Staff-Part-1/3-Let-s-Go-to-Toei-Kyoto-Studio-Park-/3-Let-s-Go-to-Toei-Kyoto-Studio-Park--231680.html)). Osawa took a different picture from the same show, a ninja circling the hero on a taut sickle-and-chain: with the lock held there is "something like a kusarigama you can't see" between Link and the foe, so pushing the stick forward closes in slowly and pushing it sideways circles round "behind your opponent, seeking for an opening." Iwata: "Then do a jump attack." Osawa: "Exactly!" The **two-Stalfos fight in the Forest Temple** was the room they built to show it off: "We were only able to pull off that scene because of that show" (same page). The three of them disagreed on the day about who remembered what, which is worth knowing when reading any of this as exact history. **Strong.**

The targeting marker became Navi. Koizumi did not want a plain upside-down triangle over the target, so he made a fairy; naming her let other ideas follow, such as her colour showing whether the target is friend or foe ([Iwata Asks Vol.2 p.4](https://www.nintendo.com/en-gb/Iwata-Asks/Iwata-Asks-The-Legend-of-Zelda-Ocarina-of-Time-3D/Vol-2-Original-Development-Staff-Part-1/4-Where-the-Name-Navi-Came-From/4-Where-the-Name-Navi-Came-From-231748.html)). Locked on, the view shifts to a letterbox, Link always faces the target, the camera follows it, and projectiles aim themselves ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Legend_of_Zelda:_Ocarina_of_Time)). **Fair.**

The game's code shows how the damage rewards that loop. Against a Stalfos (10 health), the Kokiri Sword does 1 a swing, the Master Sword 2 and the Giant's Knife 4, and **a jump attack does exactly double**: 2, 4 and 8. A blow on its shield does nothing, and the Deku Nut, boomerang and hookshot do no damage but stun it ([decompilation, z_en_test.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/zeldaret/oot/master/src/overlays/actors/ovl_En_Test/z_en_test.c)). **Strong**, for this enemy only; other enemies have their own tables.

The shallowness was a choice. Miyamoto in 1999: "The sword combat in Ocarina didn't turn out as well as I'd hoped... About a year into the development, I realized that if we lean too heavily into the sword combat, it will definitely end up being more difficult than Mario 64, so **we pulled back** and eased into the simpler system we have today" ([shmuplations](https://shmuplations.com/ocarinaoftime/)). He judged success by his fifth-grade daughter reaching each boss with one continue, and by postcards calling it "tough, but really satisfying" (same page). Later Zeldas kept the lock and added a reactive layer: Wind Waker's reviewers praised "the ability to move the camera, perform counterattacks, and use enemy items" ([Wikipedia: Wind Waker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Legend_of_Zelda:_The_Wind_Waker)), and Skyward Sword's enemies were "designed to anticipate and block Link's combat movements" ([Wikipedia: Skyward Sword](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Legend_of_Zelda:_Skyward_Sword)). That reviewers singled out counterattacks suggests Ocarina lacked them, though no source here says so outright.

The line from Z-targeting to Dark Souls and Kingdom Hearts is asserted by others, not by their makers. Wikipedia says Ocarina "popularized" target lock and context-sensitive buttons ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Legend_of_Zelda:_Ocarina_of_Time)); a blog traces Dark Souls' lock to it ([Sunset Over Ithaca](https://sunsetoverithaca.com/2019/02/03/six-years-before-breath-of-the-wild-dark-souls-reinvented-the-legend-of-zelda/)); no statement from Miyazaki or Nomura was found. One analyst argues that a too-perfect lock-on makes an attack's animation matter less than its range and speed ([critpoints](https://critpoints.net/2015/05/24/what-is-your-ideal-form-of-lock-on-in-action-games/)), but that was seen only as a summary. **Weak.**

## Ocarina's enemies read the lock-on, and its bosses began as four-line briefs

The Stalfos is the clearest case of an enemy built round Z-targeting, and the code shows it plainly. It keeps its distance (backs off inside 80 units, comes on beyond 110), and **when the player is locked on to another enemy it stays 150 units further back**. Its leaping slash from 160 to 220 units away is only chosen when it is the one locked on (30% of the time while idle, 10% while walking and blocking); its downward slash at close range mostly when it is the target, and otherwise it sidesteps. Swing at it from the side or behind and it turns, and 70% of the time jumps back, unless you have already started a jump attack ([decompilation, z_en_test.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/zeldaret/oot/master/src/overlays/actors/ovl_En_Test/z_en_test.c)). **Strong** for the numbers. The reading is one researcher's: it is a set of odds and distances keyed to "am I the target?", not a strict one-at-a-time queue, and other enemies were not checked.

The developers' own words on enemy and boss design are about process, not tells. Aonuma wrote the specs, Takizawa designed and described each creature, and Morita at SRD programmed the movement, passing versions back and forth about every fifteen minutes. For a boss, Aonuma gave only "**how the boss appears, how it attacks, how you beat it, how it ends.**" Volvagia, the lava dragon, moves on code Morita wrote for Star Fox 64's tailing fighters ([Iwata Asks Vol.3 p.3](https://www.nintendo.com/en-gb/Iwata-Asks/Iwata-Asks-The-Legend-of-Zelda-Ocarina-of-Time-3D/Vol-3-Original-Development-Staff-Part-2/3-Making-the-Bosses-and-Enemies/3-Making-the-Bosses-and-Enemies-216367.html)). Miyamoto describes how Gohma's entrance was shot to show the player where she is: her viewpoint, then Link's fear, then Link looking up at her ([Iwata Asks Vol.5 p.3](https://www.nintendo.com/en-gb/Iwata-Asks/Iwata-Asks-The-Legend-of-Zelda-Ocarina-of-Time-3D/Vol-5-Mr-Shigeru-Miyamoto/3-Everyone-Loves-Horses/3-Everyone-Loves-Horses-224665.html)). And he admits that the Megaton Hammer working on Dark Link was not fully planned: "sometimes we just run out of time to check everything," and such surprises are left in as a trust between player and maker ([shmuplations](https://shmuplations.com/ocarinaoftime/)). **Strong.**

The familiar idea that each boss tests its dungeon's new item (the slingshot on Gohma, bombs on King Dodongo and so on) is **not sourced here**; the researcher knew it but found no readable page that says it, so it should be treated as common knowledge, not evidence. The same goes for the tells of the Lizalfos, Wolfos, Iron Knuckle and the rest.

## Dungeons sized to Link's body, ramped from one puzzle to everything

Ocarina's world began as something very like RatQuest's. Miyamoto's earliest fallback was that "only having Ganon's Castle might be enough... putting all kinds of adventures into the different rooms, like making a dark meadow or an ocean, like in Princess Peach's Castle in Super Mario 64"; "in the worst case, Link wouldn't have been able to go outside the castle!" ([Iwata Asks Vol.5 p.1](https://www.nintendo.com/en-gb/Iwata-Asks/Iwata-Asks-The-Legend-of-Zelda-Ocarina-of-Time-3D/Vol-5-Mr-Shigeru-Miyamoto/1-Ganon-s-Castle-as-the-Only-Setting-/1-Ganon-s-Castle-as-the-Only-Setting-224548.html)). Instead it grew a field. Miyamoto wanted a horse; trees were cleared because they got in the way of riding; "'Just make it big,' we were told!" ([1101: Link's Horse](https://glitterberri.com/ocarina-of-time/1101-interviews/links-horse/)). The field designer: "For a long time Hyrule Field had nothing, it was terribly uninteresting." Then "lots of people took a hand in it, having enemies appear and putting holes here and there," and Aonuma "went around Hyrule Field **looking for places where there wasn't anything and put something in each one**" ([Iwata Asks Vol.3, Making the Landscape](https://www.nintendo.com/en-gb/Iwata-Asks/Iwata-Asks-The-Legend-of-Zelda-Ocarina-of-Time-3D/Vol-3-Original-Development-Staff-Part-2/5-Making-the-Landscape/5-Making-the-Landscape-216494.html)). It did not fully work: a 20th-anniversary retrospective still calls the field "painfully barren" and "very little more than a way to get from Point A to Point B" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/ocarina-of-time-20th-anniversary-retrospective)). **Strong** for intent, **fair** for the verdict.

The dungeons were made with real level metrics. "We ended up planning them like houses... **The size of the dungeon rooms was based off the size of Link's body.**" The early "living" dungeons (the Deku Tree, Jabu-Jabu's belly) have no keys, because "it would be a little weird if you needed keys in the inside of a fish"; the man-made temples do; and the Shadow and Spirit temples are "constructed with 2 sections so you can come and go as either adult or child" ([1101: Dungeons Part 1](https://glitterberri.com/ocarina-of-time/1101-interviews/dungeons/)). The difficulty curve is stated outright: "orthodox till the middle of the game, and thereafter you must put the things you've learned to use. **At the beginning, there's only one puzzle room to solve, then eventually entire dungeons become rotating puzzles. In the final dungeons, everything you've learned comes into play and the puzzles are accompanied by an athletic element**, as the staff who worked on Mario were kind enough to help us out." And to keep puzzles from frustrating, "we make sure to run the puzzle through from the beginning and **reduce the number of enemies near it**" (same page). **Strong.**

Two dungeons show the strength and the cost of a central space that changes. The Forest Temple, "the very first dungeon we designed," has at its centre a twisting corridor that, once turned, lets you "reach items you couldn't before," something only possible in 3D ([1101: Dungeons Part 2](https://glitterberri.com/ocarina-of-time/1101-interviews/dungeons-part-2/)); a critic ranks it first because "what used to be the floor is now the wall" ([Game Developer, Dungeons Ranked Part 1](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/ocarina-of-time-dungeons-ranked-part-1)). The Water Temple made the player "travel countless times to and from an area in order to unlock the solution. We were often told how stressful this was" ([1101: Dungeons Part 2](https://glitterberri.com/ocarina-of-time/1101-interviews/dungeons-part-2/)). Its problems were partly a production accident: "**The amount of time Link could stay underwater was changed part way through**, so we were working all the way up till this summer to fix situations where an item had become awkwardly positioned," and changes to the hookshot and Link's speed forced maps to be redone ([1101: Tough to Fix Part 2](https://glitterberri.com/ocarina-of-time/1101-interviews/tough-to-fix-part-2/)). Thirteen years later Aonuma was still sorry: "The worst thing was thinking about how many people may have given up there" ([Iwata Asks Vol.4 p.3](https://www.nintendo.com/en-gb/Iwata-Asks/Iwata-Asks-The-Legend-of-Zelda-Ocarina-of-Time-3D/Vol-4-Development-Staff/3-I-ve-Got-to-Fix-the-Water-Temple-/3-I-ve-Got-to-Fix-the-Water-Temple--235888.html)). Critics split on whether it was hard or just tiring to navigate ([Wikipedia: Water Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_Temple_(Ocarina_of_Time))), and Mark Brown's Boss Keys series judges a 2D Zelda's water dungeon "far easier for the player to navigate than Ocarina of Time's infamous Water Temple," while granting that 3D lets the player see how the floating platforms work ([Game Developer on Boss Keys](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/take-a-close-look-at-the-level-design-of-some-exceptional-i-zelda-i-games)). By contrast the Fire Temple's large rooms lack landmarks and it is "easy to get turned around" ([Game Developer, Part 2](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/ocarina-of-time-dungeons-ranked-part-2)).

Miyamoto also turned away from mazes: "Instead of mapping your way through a maze... a sense of dread, a sense of pressure, and of course an opportunity for finding secrets and solving puzzles"; traditional mazes are "not very appropriate to a 3D game" ([shmuplations](https://shmuplations.com/ocarinaoftime/)). He supervised "everything up through the first Deku Tree dungeon, like where you destroy the spiderweb and jump down" himself, adding "little traps and puzzles that, once solved, make the player feel like 'Ah, now this is a Zelda game'" (same page).

On rewards, the dungeon designer gives a lesson that fits the owner's complaint almost word for word. There were "lots of rupee-filled treasure chests at first, but **people ended up getting annoyed wondering why there were so many rupees in a place where they were totally useless**." The hardest question was "what could we put in, at what point in time, in what place, that would make the player happy?" Early dungeons had grass full of hearts to avoid early frustration; later, a "limited supply" ([1101: Dungeons Part 2](https://glitterberri.com/ocarina-of-time/1101-interviews/dungeons-part-2/)). **Strong.** The criticism that the 100 Gold Skulltulas pay out a poor reward (200 rupees) was seen only in search summaries ([ScreenRant](https://screenrant.com/zelda-ocarina-time-gold-skulltula-reward-bad-stupid/)). **Weak.**

Navi was there because the camera hides things: in 3D "it's important that the player be able to see what routes to follow and not overlook something important. By changing the camera viewpoint just a little you might not be able to see something you ought" ([1101: Half Done by Mario](https://glitterberri.com/ocarina-of-time/1101-interviews/half-done-by-mario/)). Yet Miyamoto called "the whole system with Navi giving you advice... the biggest weakpoint of Ocarina of Time" ([shmuplations](https://shmuplations.com/ocarinaoftime/)). **Strong.**

## The Mario team gave up the jump button to buy authored motion

Ocarina had a jump button at first. Koizumi says that in Mario 64 "the action hurdle was a high one... if you tried to beat an enemy in front of you, the axes weren't aligned," and that while making Mario they talked about "decreasing the action element in The Legend of Zelda and increasing the puzzle elements" ([Iwata Asks Vol.2 p.2](https://www.nintendo.com/en-gb/Iwata-Asks/Iwata-Asks-The-Legend-of-Zelda-Ocarina-of-Time-3D/Vol-2-Original-Development-Staff-Part-1/2-The-Legend-of-Zelda-with-Chanbara-style-Action/2-The-Legend-of-Zelda-with-Chanbara-style-Action-231626.html)). Miyamoto thought of auto-jump on a day off and announced it on Monday; "Everyone's response was, 'What?!' **The team that had made Mario games was going to give up the jump button.**" What it bought: "we could program the pose Link would assume after jumping... look at the landform ahead and determine how Link would jump," such as a headfirst dive off a high waterfall, and from that came the feeling of height: dive and you are diving, "but if you raised your arms high and jumped feet first... you're just a falling person" ([Iwata Asks Vol.5 p.5](https://www.nintendo.com/en-gb/Iwata-Asks/Iwata-Asks-The-Legend-of-Zelda-Ocarina-of-Time-3D/Vol-5-Mr-Shigeru-Miyamoto/5-A-Sword-Sorcery-Tale-Admired-Worldwide/5-A-Sword-Sorcery-Tale-Admired-Worldwide-224778.html)). In 1999 he called it "a new 'grammar' for jumping," written into the terrain, and said a jump button would be "one-too-many gameplay elements" ([shmuplations](https://shmuplations.com/ocarinaoftime/)). Even the horse jumps by itself because, Osawa recalls, Miyamoto said "a Legend of Zelda game doesn't need any difficult actions" ([Iwata Asks Vol.2 p.5](https://www.nintendo.com/en-gb/Iwata-Asks/Iwata-Asks-The-Legend-of-Zelda-Ocarina-of-Time-3D/Vol-2-Original-Development-Staff-Part-1/5-What-We-Couldn-t-Do-with-Ocarina-of-Time/5-What-We-Couldn-t-Do-with-Ocarina-of-Time-231818.html)). **Strong.** Aonuma's reported view that auto-jump is "convenient" because it controls where players can go was seen only as a summary ([Nintendo World Report](https://nintendoworldreport.com/news/26926)). **Weak.**

So the stated reasons were three: new things to show (poses chosen by the ground, a sense of height), 3D action being hard to control, and Miyamoto's rule against difficult actions. The cost is the player's choice of when and how far to jump, which is the heart of Mario 64 and of what the owner wants. The series kept auto-jump through Twilight Princess and Skyward Sword ([Wikipedia: Twilight Princess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Legend_of_Zelda:_Twilight_Princess); [Wikipedia: Skyward Sword](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Legend_of_Zelda:_Skyward_Sword)) and replaced it in Breath of the Wild with climbing almost anything, limited by stamina ([Wikipedia: Breath of the Wild](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Legend_of_Zelda:_Breath_of_the_Wild)).

The freed button went to context actions: one button pushes a box when Link stands by it but climbs it when the stick points at it, and the screen shows what it will do. Reviewers rated this among the game's strongest ideas but said it "occasionally caused the player to perform unintended actions" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Legend_of_Zelda:_Ocarina_of_Time)). **Fair.**

The camera was largely automatic. Wikipedia reports, citing Miyamoto, that Ocarina's camera "is much more limited and is largely controlled by the AI," reflecting a focus on the world where Mario 64's camera focused on the character, and that the engine began as Mario 64's but was "so heavily modified" that Miyamoto considers them different ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Legend_of_Zelda:_Ocarina_of_Time)). Pressing Z with nothing to target brings the view directly behind Link ([Iwata Asks Vol.2 p.3](https://www.nintendo.com/en-gb/Iwata-Asks/Iwata-Asks-The-Legend-of-Zelda-Ocarina-of-Time-3D/Vol-2-Original-Development-Staff-Part-1/3-Let-s-Go-to-Toei-Kyoto-Studio-Park-/3-Let-s-Go-to-Toei-Kyoto-Studio-Park--231680.html), interview footnote). By the 3DS remake in 2011 one reviewer called the once-revolutionary lock-on imprecise and the camera clumsy ([Game Developer roundup](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/critical-reception-nintendo-s-i-zelda-ocarina-of-time-3d-i-), summary only). **Weak.**

Two sets of numbers matter. Speedrunners measured adult Link walking at 9 units a frame and **rolling or walking backwards at 13.5, half again as fast as walking**, the fastest normal way to move ([ZeldaSpeedRuns](https://www.zeldaspeedruns.com/oot/generalknowledge/movement-speeds)), which explains why players roll everywhere. **Fair** (community measurement). And the code sets fall damage in bands: under 200 units of drop nothing, 200 to 400 only a rumble, 400 to 800 a small hurt, 800 or more twice that, with 40 frames of safety after; and **landing from a drop of more than 80 with the stick held forward turns into an automatic roll** that skips the heavy landing ([decompilation, z_player.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/zeldaret/oot/master/src/overlays/actors/ovl_player_actor/z_player.c)). **Strong** for the thresholds; how many hearts "a small hurt" is (probably half a heart) is a reading of the code, moderate. Jump distances, ledge reach and turning speeds were not found.

### What the 2026 remake changed (remake-era sources, kept apart)

Wikipedia reports that the Switch 2 remake, released on 5 November 2026, has a freely controllable camera and lets Link sprint and **jump by hand**; it cites a GamesRadar+ article whose headline says the remake "fixes rolling spam and adds the jump button players demanded 28 years ago" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Legend_of_Zelda:_Ocarina_of_Time)). The article itself was not read. Game Developer (2 November 2026) reports Aonuma saying he wanted today's team to learn "the game structure and development style" of the original, and that recent Zeldas moved away from "visiting dungeons in a linear path and collecting principal items" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/production/zelda-producer-ocarina-of-time-remake-helps-junior-staff-learn-classic-insights)). This says nothing about why the 1998 team chose what it chose, but it does show Nintendo, with modern hardware, moving towards the very choices RatQuest has already made: a jump button, a player-held camera and a roll that is not the fastest way to travel.

## What this means for RatQuest

RatQuest already has more in common with Ocarina than either game has with Mario 64. Its lock-on file says outright it is "in the style of Zelda 64" (`src/combat/lockon.ts`): the nearest foe near the middle of the screen, the rat facing him while he walks so that he circles, swings aimed at him, a flick of the right stick to switch, and with nobody near, the view brought round behind him, which is Ocarina's Z press exactly. Its director (`src/enemies/director.ts`) lets one enemy attack at a time, two when four or more are close, with 0.7 seconds between attacks, which is Koizumi's Kyoto lesson arrived at independently. Its Club, a room of doors to worlds, is Miyamoto's first plan for Ocarina. Where the games part is the very place the owner wants them to: Ocarina pulled its combat back to be easier than Mario 64 and took away the jump; RatQuest has a 0.16-second perfect guard, poise, heavy blows a shield cannot stop, enemies that punish mashing, and a jump, a double jump, a long jump, a wall kick and a plunge. So the rule for everything below is: **take Ocarina's structure and staging, never its easing off.** None of these suggestions makes the game easier; where one could be read that way it says so and says how to keep it hard.

### Combat and the lock-on

**Make the lock-on matter to the enemies, the way it matters to the Stalfos.** At present the director gives the next attack to whichever waiting enemy is nearest; whether the rat is locked on to it makes no difference to anything the enemy does. Ocarina's Stalfos picks its most dangerous move, the leaping slash, only when it is the target, and hangs back when another is. The RatQuest version that fits the owner's wish: the enemy he is locked on to gets first claim on the attack turn and becomes likelier to use its biggest committed move (the mantis's leap, the guard's thrust, the hornet's dive), while the ones he is not locked on to do not simply wait in a ring but move to his flanks and back, as the Ant Pikemen's FLANK already does, ready to take the director's second slot. Locking on becomes a choice with a cost (it makes that enemy fight harder and tells the others where you are looking), not a free aiming aid. Rests on the decompiled Stalfos and Koizumi's account. **Strong** for what Ocarina does; the "lock as permission to attack" reading is one researcher's interpretation of one enemy.

**Do not move the lock to a new enemy by itself when one dies.** `keepTarget` in `lockon.ts` picks another foe within 9 units when the locked one falls, and the view then swings round to keep that one in sight. That is the camera turning without the player asking, which the owner has ruled out, and Koizumi's own words are that "the moment you beat that one, *you* can switch the Z Targeting to the next opponent": the player chooses. Let the lock drop on a kill (or hold the view still and show the next marker until the right stick or the lock button confirms it). Rests on Koizumi (**strong**) and the owner's camera rule; whether Ocarina itself moves the lock on a kill is not documented here.

**Give the locked-on jump a committed cut, and pay it for the risk.** Ocarina doubles a sword's damage for the jump attack (1 to 2, 2 to 4, 4 to 8), and Osawa's whole picture of the lock ends "then do a jump attack." RatQuest's swings do 1, 1 and 2 across the combo, and its only air attack is the plunge (3 on the way down, 2 in the blast), which drops straight down. A forward leaping cut, pressed as attack early in a jump while locked on, could do 2 at once and leave him stuck as long as the plunge does (0.5 s) if it misses or is blocked. The jump itself must stay a jump when locked on, for the Mario feel; only jump-then-attack changes. This is a Dark Souls kind of trade: a big blow with a big opening. Rests on the Stalfos damage table and Osawa (**strong**). It adds a move, so it is a suggestion to try on the sliders, not a fix.

**Keep the lock imperfect.** The rat's swings already turn towards a foe within 3.4 units without any lock, and the lock keeps him facing it. RatQuest's newer enemies already argue with that: the mantis hops back the moment a swing starts, the Keep Guard's shield turns at 5.5 radians a second (too fast to circle), and the Stag Knight stomps anyone who has done the Ocarina thing and circled behind it. That is the right answer to Osawa's invisible chain: the circle is a tool some enemies punish. Do not add homing to swings or lunges, and keep checking in the tests that a locked-on rat can still miss a dodging mantis. Rests on the critpoints argument that a perfect lock makes animation matter less (**weak**, summary only) and on the owner's Souls direction.

### Enemies and bosses

**Give every new insect a first meeting built for it, as the two-Stalfos room was built for the lock-on.** The Hornet Lancer already has one: the Foundry dock, open floor, nothing else about, none of it over lava. Do the same for the others where they are first met. The Stag Knight stands on the Crucible's island among the six enemies of "The Slag Crew"; the mantis on the Webbed Gallery among the gallery's seven; the Ant Pikemen on the Hollows' north floor are already a showcase for the pair. One room per enemy where it is the only thing happening, and only later rooms that mix them. Rests on Koizumi's two-Stalfos scene (**strong**).

**Write each boss on Aonuma's four lines: how it appears, how it attacks, how you beat it, how it ends.** The Broodmother, the Sump Wyrm and Grimsqueak all have the middle two in detail and the last one (a wedge where the boss falls), but only Grimsqueak has an entrance; the other two have none. A wordless entrance of a few seconds, shot the way Miyamoto shot Gohma to show where she is, would fit a silent hero: for the Broodmother especially, whose angry phase climbs to the roof on a thread, showing her on the roof as he enters plants the idea that the roof is hers before she uses it. When it ends the view should be behind him and then stay where it is; it must not turn during play. This is presentation, not story, but it sits near the parked story, so it is the owner's call. Rests on Aonuma's spec and Miyamoto on Gohma (**strong**).

**Let each boss test what its world taught, above all the wall kick.** Ocarina's designer says the final dungeons bring "everything you've learned" together with an athletic element (**strong**); the idea that each boss tests its dungeon's item is well known but **not sourced** here. RatQuest's versions: the Cistern is now the wall-kick world (three climbs where it had one), so the Sump off it could have a chimney or a pair of pillars from which a rat can get above the Wyrm and plunge onto its head, which takes double while it is stunned against a wall. That answers HANDOFF's open worry that a rat circling close can stay off its head, and it asks for a skill rather than handing out an easy route, because the Wyrm keeps moving and the climb has to be done mid-fight. The Broodmother's pit could test the Hollows' Leap of Faith in the same way.

**Leave in surprising answers that still ask for skill.** Miyamoto kept the hammer working on Dark Link as a "trust" between player and maker (**strong**). The plunge striking a Firefly Bomber out of the air from a double jump is that kind of answer; keep it. Close only the ones that skip a fight or a route without effort, as the Cistern work already does for short cuts.

### Levels and how they connect

**Settle the move set's numbers before the Foundry, the Hollows and the Keep are rebuilt.** Ocarina's dungeon team spent its last summer redoing the Water Temple because Link's underwater time, hookshot and speed changed after the maps were made. RatQuest is in the same spot: the Super Mario 64 study (`research_notes/README.md`) found the knight jumps far higher for his size than Mario (2.3 body heights from a standstill, 4.3 with the double jump, against Mario's 1.4) and links that to the owner's complaint that he "can just jump over stuff." If the double jump is going to be lowered, lower it first, because every wall height in the remaining levels is set against what he can reach. The jump checker in `src/levels/reach.ts` and the route tests in `tests/cistern.ts` are the right guard; they only help if the numbers stop moving. Rests on the dungeon designer's "Tough to Fix" account (**strong**).

**Ramp the four worlds the way Ocarina ramped its dungeons.** "One puzzle room, then entire dungeons become rotating puzzles, then everything you've learned plus an athletic element." For RatQuest: the Cistern teaches one move per place (it now does: a chimney here, a moving-platform ride there); the Foundry and the Hollows build whole areas round one idea each; the Keep combines them, with wall-kick climbs that have fights on them. The designer's other rule, fewer enemies near a puzzle, belongs to the first half of that curve: keep the Cistern's and the Foundry's wall-kick climbs free of attackers so the climb is the test, and mix the two only in the Keep, on purpose. That is not easier, because the last world gets harder. Right now the mixing happens by accident in places: the mantis in Web Hollow can knock him off the middle floor into the drop, and a Firefly hangs over the Keep's inner ward ledge with the pit in front; decide whether each is meant. Rests on the dungeon designer (**strong**).

**Give each world one central space that changes, and make the change quick to see.** The Forest Temple's twisting hall and the Water Temple's water level are what made each feel like one place; the Fire Temple's landmark-less rooms are the cautionary case. RatQuest's worlds have natural centres: the Cistern's tower, the Foundry's Crucible, the Hollows' Webbed Gallery, the Keep's courtyard. Taking a wedge could change the centre (a sluice drops, a bridge swings, a lift starts) and open a new route there. The Water Temple shows the price: a change that takes many slow trips to toggle becomes a chore, and its designer was still apologising thirteen years later. One change per wedge, seen from where it is made, never a switch to flip back and forth. Rests on the designer's own words (**strong**) and critics (**fair**).

**Keep the Club small, and fill empty stretches with routes, not pickups.** Hyrule Field was made big first and filled later with "holes here and there," and it is still called barren. RatQuest's Club is already the compact, Peach's-Castle shape Miyamoto first planned, so leave it that way rather than grow a field between worlds. Inside the worlds, Aonuma's walk round "looking for places where there wasn't anything" is a good habit, with one change for this owner: what goes in an empty place is a climb, a jump or a fight, not a crumb. HANDOFF asks whether the Cistern's eight things are "too few for a hall this size"; the Hyrule Field lesson says the answer is more things to do there, not more things to pick up. Rests on the field designers (**strong**) and the retrospective (**fair**).

**Show every gear secret on the first visit.** Ocarina's two-section temples and the Forest Temple's "reach items you couldn't before" are the same idea as RatQuest's gear secrets (the Sludge Scarf's drain, the Coal Bin's iron-bound crate, the Pyre's fire). The weak spot is seeing them: the dungeon designer says Navi exists because a small change of view can hide what you ought to see, and START-HERE warns that the game's camera sees only about 5 to 7 units up a wall. The Cleft's cap sits ten units up. With a silent hero and no Navi (Miyamoto called Navi the game's "biggest weakpoint"), the level must do the showing: a glint or a hanging thing at the foot of each climb, or a spot across the room from which the prize can be seen. Rests on the designer and Miyamoto (**strong**) and the game's own camera limit.

### Collectibles

**Make every reward useful where it is found.** "Why there were so many rupees in a place where they were totally useless" is the owner's "like Pac-Man" complaint from 1998. RatQuest's hunks of cheese are shop money, so place them so that a world's hunks buy the gear whose secret is in that world; `tests/balance.ts` already checks that all gear is affordable from the worlds but not from the Cistern alone, which is the right shape. A bottle cap counts towards a wedge, so it should sit at the end of a route as hard as the wedge it feeds. Rests on the dungeon designer (**strong**). Avoid a large count hunt with a small prize at the end, the criticism made of the Gold Skulltulas (**weak**, summaries only).

**Plenty of healing early, a limited supply late.** Ocarina put grass full of hearts in its first dungeons and rationed them later. RatQuest's healing is the nacho can, now dropped by one crate in ten everywhere. The Ocarina rule, applied the hard way round, is to keep that rate in the Cistern and lower it in the Keep, not to raise it anywhere. Rests on the designer (**strong**).

### Movement

**Keep the jump, and take auto-jump's two real gifts another way.** Miyamoto's reasons for auto-jump were poses chosen by the ground and the feeling of height. RatQuest can have the second without losing control. Right now a fall in RatQuest costs nothing unless it ends in a pit or lava; Ocarina hurts Link for long drops (nothing under 200 units, a rumble to 400, a hurt beyond, twice that beyond 800) and turns a landing with the stick held forward into a roll that saves him the heavy landing. A RatQuest version: a big drop costs a heart unless he rolls as he lands, with the dodge button pressed in a short window. That makes the way down part of the route, gives height weight, and is harder, not easier. Rests on the decompiled fall code (**strong**; the size of the hurt is a moderate reading). It changes what the jump checker must allow, so it is best decided along with the jump numbers above.

**Make sure the roll is not the fastest way to travel.** Ocarina's roll was 1.5 times walking speed, so players rolled everywhere, and the 2026 remake reportedly fixes "rolling spam" (headline only). RatQuest's numbers read as if the same thing could happen on a smaller scale: the roll runs at 7.0 against a run of 6.2, it lasts 0.5 s, and its 0.5 s wait seems to start when a roll starts, which would let rolls be chained back to back, faster than running and safe from hits for most of each roll. Try it by hand; if it is so, start the wait when the roll ends or slow the roll below the run. Rests on the speedrun measurements (**fair**) and the remake headline (**weak**, remake-era).

**Watch the button that both jumps and talks.** The jump button talks when he is beside someone with something to say (`main.ts`). Ocarina's reviewers liked its one-button context actions but noted "unintended actions." Keep characters such as Finch away from the foot of jumps, or allow talk only when he is standing still, so a jump is never swallowed by a conversation. Rests on Wikipedia's reception summary (**fair**).

**Make the wall jump the "athletic element."** Ocarina's dungeon designer credits the Mario staff for the platforming in the final dungeons. In RatQuest the Mario part is the movement, and the owner wants far more wall jumps. Following the ramp above: the Foundry and the Hollows each get their own chimneys and wall-to-wall kicks across gaps (the HANDOFF rules hold: walls 2 to 2.5 apart, a 2.4-thick landing at the top, five units from any other wall), and the Keep's best things sit at the top of climbs that join a wall kick to a fight or a moving platform. Rests on the designer's ramp (**strong**) and the owner's own words.

### Camera

**Keep the player's camera; Ocarina's automatic one is the thing not to copy.** Ocarina's camera was mostly driven by the game because the N64 pad had no spare stick, and by 2011 a reviewer called it clumsy; the 2026 remake, by Wikipedia's account, gives the camera to the player. RatQuest's rule (only the right stick turns the view; the lock button with nobody near brings it behind him) already takes the one good part, Ocarina's Z press to recentre, and leaves the rest. The one place to tidy is the lock moving to a new enemy on a kill, above. Ocarina's letterbox while locked on would cost screen height on a phone held sideways, and the game already shows the lock with a marker, so it is not worth adding. Rests on Wikipedia's account of the camera (**fair**) and the remake (**remake-era**).

The short version: Ocarina is the closest model RatQuest has for how to **stage** a fight and **build** a place, and the weakest model for how hard a fight should be or how the hero should move. Its team found, through a samurai show, that a fair fight against a crowd is a scripted queue; RatQuest found the same by itself, and its next step is to let the enemies know who the player is looking at. Its dungeon designer gives a ramp, a rule about rewards and a warning about changing the hero's numbers late, all three of which bear on the level work under way now. And its most famous choice, the missing jump button, is the clearest sign that the owner is right to go the other way: Nintendo has just put the jump back.
