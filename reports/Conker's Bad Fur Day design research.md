# Conker's bar is staged, not simulated: fill the Club the same way

Conker's Bad Fur Day (Rare, Nintendo 64, 2001) is the game the owner named when he asked for the Club to feel "like the bar at the beginning of Conker's Bad Fur Day". The first thing the research found is that **the bar is not a room you play in**. It is the opening scene, the closing scene (where it stands empty), and, by second-hand report, the backdrop of the main menu. The place you walk about in between levels is a different one, an outdoor hub called Windy, and it feels lived-in for a plain reason: almost every landmark in it is a character with a job to give or a door to open ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conker%27s_Bad_Fur_Day)). Nothing that could be read describes anyone in either place wandering, deciding or simulating anything. The life is **authored**: voices recorded first, animation fitted to them, small reactions to what the player does, music and footsteps that change with where he stands, and about two hours of cutscenes where the makers, not the player, hold the timing. Its "context-sensitive" pads are one button that does whatever a marked spot is for, and its only collectible is money. For RATQUEST this comes to three things. The Club as built on 2026-11-16 is already made the way Conker's rooms were (staged loops, one spot each, a room that is full from the first minute); what it lacks is what Conker's team spent their care on, which is **small reactions, staged arrivals and sound**. Our hero is silent, so the half of Conker's method that is voice cannot be copied; the comedy has to be carried by bodies, timing and the room's reaction to an ordinary knight, and the sources that help most there are not Conker at all. And pads suit the Club, where nothing needs skill, and do not suit the levels, where they would do the knight's moving for him. One caution runs through everything: **no interview with the people who made the game could be read, and the game itself could not be watched**. What follows rests mostly on Wikipedia's long, well-cited article, and on search summaries for exactly the part the owner cares most about, the inside of the bar.

## What could be read, and how much to trust it

The research was done in two passes on 2026-11-16, both under a network that allows only a few sites. This report keeps three kinds of source apart.

**Read in full, but second-hand about the makers.** Wikipedia's article on the game is the backbone ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conker%27s_Bad_Fur_Day)). Its development section is long and cites the 2013 director's commentary by Chris Seavor (project lead, designer, most of the voices), Robin Beanland (composer and co-writer) and the programmers Shawn Pile and Chris Marlow, the 2015 Rare Replay "making of" film, the Nintendo Power guide, the manual, and the 2001 reviews. None of those could be opened or heard here. So when this report says "Seavor said", it means "Wikipedia reports that Seavor said". The second pass re-read the article from its raw text and checked every quotation the notes mark as read against the pages by machine; all were exact. Also read: Wikipedia on the 2005 remake, the series, Rare, Banjo-Kazooie and Donkey Kong 64, and on Mos Eisley (the Star Wars cantina, the owner's other reference for the Club).

**Read in full, first-hand, but not about Conker.** Game Developer's own search turns up no interview with the Conker team. What it has, and what was read: a 2007 interview with two Rare staff who mention the game in passing; a piece in which the artists of Banjo-Kazooie describe making characters on the Nintendo 64; an interview with the two makers of Thank Goodness You're Here!, a 2024 comedy game whose small hero does everything with one slap; a feature in which three comedy-game writers talk about timing; the Psychonauts postmortem's account of its cutscene editor; an animator's account of Little Kitty, Big City; and a few blog essays on context-sensitive actions and on scenes without words. These are used where Conker's method does not fit a silent hero, and each is named where it is used.

**Search summaries only (second-hand, weak).** Everything about what is physically inside the pub (it is called the Cock and Plucker), the pub as the main menu, the names of Windy's residents, the hub turning to night, the button layout, and the light bulb over Conker's head at a pad comes from a search tool's short summaries of fan wikis, Giant Bomb, TV Tropes and walkthroughs. Those pages were refused by the network. Each such claim below is marked **second-hand**.

**Not read at all:** Seavor's and Marlow's own interviews (Kotaku, Nintendo Life, Scouse Gamer 88, Splice Today, RareGamer, Retro Gamer, Eurogamer), the commentary and the film (video), the Conker wiki, the manual, the 2001 reviews themselves, and the game. `research_notes/README.md` lists what to ask for if more sites can be allowed; the three notes files in `research_notes/Conker's Bad Fur Day design research/` give every finding with its address and an exact quote.

## The bar is a scene, a menu and an epilogue

What Wikipedia supports is short. The whole plot starts from "a night of binge drinking with his friends before they are drafted for a war"; the opening "begins with a shot-by-shot recreation of the intro of A Clockwork Orange", and it is the only written scene in the game: "Out of all the game's dialogue, only the intro was scripted." After the credits the game goes back: Conker "is seen back at the same pub he was drinking in at the start of the game, now barren of all his friends due to their deaths in the war", and he "drunkenly exits the bar, leaving in the opposite direction he took previously" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conker%27s_Bad_Fur_Day)). Those are the only sentences about the pub in the article. It does not name the pub, describe its inside, or mention the menu.

The rest is **second-hand**. Search summaries of fan pages say the pub is also the main menu, with the menu's choices laid out as rooms of the pub (chapters in the cloak room, multiplayer in a store room at the back); that the menu shows Conker walking into a bar full of grey squirrel soldiers, with a few named characters from the game among them; that there is a bartender; and that the official guide printed Conker's bar tab from the opening. One summary quotes a fan page contrasting the empty pub of the ending with the pub that, in the opening and the menu, "was humming with life". Nobody here has seen any of it.

Two things follow, and both are our reading. First, **the bar's fullness is a staged crowd**: a cutscene, one camera path, one piece of music, and drinkers who, if the summaries are right, are mostly grey squirrel soldiers of one build. Nothing suggests they have behaviour. The owner's memory of that bar is probably the opening and the menu together, and what he remembers is the right thing to remember: a room of people busy with their own evening. Second, **a room's head count is a storytelling tool that players feel at once**. Conker uses it exactly once, in the sad direction: full, then empty. The owner wants the other direction (a full room that gains extras), and his rule that the Club must be full from the first minute is the right guard against the obvious mistake, which would be to start with Conker's ending.

### The other room he named: the cantina

The owner's first comparison was "the Star Wars Cantina", and how that room was filled can be read ([Wikipedia, Mos Eisley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mos_Eisley)). The crowd was designed as a set of types first: the costume designer worked with George Lucas "to compile a chart of visual designs for a range of character types", and masks were made to match. The set was "complete with circular bar and shady alcoves": one centre, many corners. A band is part of the furniture, played by crew who "all mimed to" a swing record. The scene was planned as a shock by contrast, since the film had shown few creatures until then. And the first shoot did not make the room full enough: the fix, shot later, was "several close-ups of alien creatures" cut into it. The novelisation describes the room by what the drinkers' hands are doing: "Tentacles, claws and hands... wrapped around drinking utensils". Again, nobody wanders and nothing is simulated. It is types chosen for contrast, each doing one thing with its hands, a band, a barman with a house rule, and a few faces seen close.

## The hub you actually walk is full of characters who are doors

"Windy is the game's main hub with entrances to most other sections", and each area "has a distinct colour theme". The way the game plays there and everywhere is stated in one line: "The gameplay mostly relies on figuring out a way to help other characters by completing a linear sequence of challenges", and "The result is always a cash reward, which aids access to other areas" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conker%27s_Bad_Fur_Day)).

What makes the hub feel inhabited, on the evidence read:

- **A resident is a destination.** A queen bee whose hive has been stolen; a dung beetle at a cabin who greets Conker ("Would you like to come in now? Yeah, sit down. Wa d'ya want?") and sets him a job; a barrel who must be paid ("$2,110 is needed to pay Mr. Barrel") to smash the way into a late level; weasel guards who take $1,000 at another door. The prices are on people, not on signs.
- **The hub opens in steps, and the steps are things that happen to the place.** One part of it is "accessible after completing Barn Boys"; another "is only opened once the water in the river is drained". **Second-hand:** the first job in the hub (carry the hive back) comes round again much later with a twist, and late in the game the hub turns to night and stays that way for the last three chapters.
- **The game knows its bystanders stand still, and makes a joke of it.** Burt, a metal box, "stands in place for most of the level, is only there to open a gate", which Wikipedia reads as poking "fun at characters in other games only there to be communicated with".

None of this needs a character who walks about. Each one needs a place, a look, a voice and one thing to want.

## A cast of animals, objects and enemies who talk

The creatures are a mix: talking animals (squirrels, weasels, a panther king, a catfish, cows), **talking objects** (a metal box, a barrel, and by second-hand report a pitchfork, a paint pot, a brush, cogs and the wads of money themselves), and film-parody villains (teddy-bear soldiers, a vampire, an alien) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conker%27s_Bad_Fur_Day)). Three patterns matter for the Club.

**An enemy kind is a hazard; one of its members is a person.** Weasels are a mafia to fear and also two guards who can be bought and a boss who hires Conker twice before betraying him. Dung beetles block a path (second-hand) and one of them is a chatty host in a cabin. In multiplayer, enemy kinds become playable sides. This is the shape the owner asked for when he said an ant "can also just kind of be a chill dude drinking", and Corporal Nib and Sir Hobnail already follow it.

**Three people voiced everyone, so each group got one loud idea.** Apart from one boss, "all of the male characters, including Conker, and two female characters were voiced by Seavor", the rest of the women by the animator Louise Ridgeway, and that boss, who sings opera, by a programmer "who had experience in opera". The character work is done by an accent or a tic per group: "the New York accent on the Nasty Wasps", cavemen who speak in "random grunts and fast word sayings", a girlfriend whose actress, told to sound American, could only think "to add 'like' so often". Rare's earlier Banjo-Kazooie did the same job with mumbles and text, a choice made "to convey their personalities without them actually speaking", with one voice reused "pitch-shifted down to save space" ([Wikipedia, Banjo-Kazooie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banjo-Kazooie_(video_game))). That game's designer, Gregg Mayles, gave the rule for the whole cast: all the characters "basically had something wrong with them".

**Faces were the showpiece, and nobody explains how.** Reviewers singled out "detailed facial animations, lip syncing, and individually rendered fingers on some characters"; IGN's reviewer wrote that "When he's scared, he looks it". Dialogue also appeared in speech bubbles, timed by hand. What this cost on that machine is told by Banjo's own artist: "every extra joint to animate was a performance hit", so Banjo had "no way of moving his mouth" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/-i-yooka-laylee-i-devs-7-biggest-game-design-changes-since-the-n64-era)). How Conker's lip sync was made was not found. The owner has already ruled mouths out for RATQUEST ("we don't have to actually like animate their mouths"), so this gap does not matter here.

The same Game Developer piece gives two numbers worth keeping for a low-polygon game. A character then "could be concepted, modeled and animated in a week", from "around 500 polygons". And a level designer's remark: on old machines "only important bits could afford to have eye-catching detail", which told the player where to look.

## The pads: one button that does whatever the spot is for

"The game employs 'context-sensitive' pads that allow Conker to gain different, temporary abilities when pressing the 'B' button atop them." The first one is the tutorial and a joke at once: he "drinks some Alka-Seltzer to relieve his hangover". Others "turn Conker into an anvil" or "pull out his shotgun, blow torch, throwing knives, and slingshot". And: "They also serve to inform players of what needs to be done next." They go with a deliberately small set of ordinary moves (run, jump, a frying pan, swim, climb, push): "Conker's abilities are simpler" than those of Rare's earlier heroes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conker%27s_Bad_Fur_Day)). **Second-hand:** a light bulb appears over his head on a pad, and Giant Bomb's page is summarised as tracing the idea to the context-sensitive A button of Ocarina of Time.

No statement by Rare on why they made the pads could be found. The nearest is Seavor on what he was guarding as project lead: "Just doing a 'thing' like hitting something with a brick is far more engaging if there's a motivation behind it to disguise the binary nature of the act". "Binary" is the telling word: a pad action is one press, on or off, and the story around it is what makes it worth pressing. Rare had used pads before in a narrower way: in Banjo-Kazooie, flying and the high spring jump "can only be activated by standing on pads signifying them". Conker's pads look like that idea widened from "this spot allows move X" to "this spot allows whatever the scene needs" (our reading).

The pads served a formula that Seavor set with his first act as lead. A mission about wasps stealing a beehive had no ending, so "He decided to end it with the beehive having guns shooting at the wasps"; the studio's founders told the team to "make more of that". "This set the formula for later missions: an introduction, interaction of the mission, and then an 'extreme punchline' cutscene as reward for completing the task."

Critics split, and the split is instructive. For: IGN said the pads "help keep the action shifting, refreshing, and always exciting" and "credited Rare for reducing the number of items to collect"; GameRevolution liked "a staccato flow between gameplay and cutscenes". Against: Edge called the game "little more than a procession of barely connected and puerile minigames"; Next Generation's reviewer "felt that many puzzles lack logical coherence and depended too much on trial and error" and was bothered by "the game's lack of clear direction on where to go". (All as quoted by Wikipedia; the reviews were not read.)

General design writing, read in full, says when a one-off action like this works. A designer writing on Game Developer gives three rules: show the goal, use the buttons the player already knows, keep it quick; and warns that "using a context-sensitive interaction once is too rare" ([Hallaran](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/organically-teaching-and-implementing-context-sensitive-mechanics)). Another: "Inputs should never be contextually useless", meaning the button must always answer, even with a "no" ([Hemsley](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/don-t-surprise-me-or-preventing-input-related-frustration)). A critic who dislikes most such things allows the kind where "you're not locked out of your other options" and which "consistently use the same inputs" ([CritPoints](https://critpoints.net/2015/03/19/making-good-qtes/)). Conker passes most of these (the pad is the visible goal, the button is always B, and B swings the pan anywhere else) and fails the first, which is where Edge's complaint lands: many pad actions are used once and never again. The honest cost of a pad is that **it has no skill in it**. The fun is the surprise and the staging, so it cannot carry platforming or a fight.

## One road of sketches, and one thing to collect

"Each level is an enclosed area in which the player can freely explore to find tasks to do", but the tasks run in a fixed order: in Wikipedia's account of GameRevolution's review, "there's only one path to traverse the game's big world". The game is closer to a sketch show strung on a hub than to Mario 64 (our reading; the makers of a later comedy game use the very phrase, below). Money is the only thing collected. It is a key (areas "are initially blocked off until Conker earns a certain amount of cash") and a joke about the hero's greed, and, **second-hand**, the game stops handing it out once the last gate is paid for, two chapters before the end. Health is floating chocolate, and the game "makes fun of floating collect-able items in other games". Lives are explained by a character, a Grim Reaper met on first dying ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conker%27s_Bad_Fur_Day)).

This was a turn away from Rare's own habit, and the history is on the owner's side. Sixteen months earlier Rare had shipped Donkey Kong 64, whose "strong emphasis on collectibles was a design choice made at the request of" a founder; its director recalls bringing him more and being told "No, more things". Reviewers then "criticized its camera and emphasis on item collection and backtracking", one calling it "an interactive egg hunt", and in time "Rare's 3D platformers became notorious for their emphasis on collecting items" ([Wikipedia, Donkey Kong 64](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donkey_Kong_64)). Conker's one currency was praised by name for being less. The owner's own words ("less collectibles and each collectible should be more rewarding to get") are the same lesson, reached by playing.

Other points of structure, briefly. The scenarios became film parodies by accident (a Terminator joke in a barn) and then by method: "The parodies helped the developers come up with ideas for music, sound, design, and gameplay." The team found that "being less focused on gameplay and more on the comedic premises made the levels come together easier". The late game changes kind, "its later sections featuring elements of shooters". Bosses "take four hits to kill, a twist on the typical three". And the camera was a choice made for looks that cost the play: "To make the game look cinematic, Rare went with having a fixed camera that was very zoomed out", and reviewers said it did "not allow players to properly judge their position" and made the character "difficult to control, especially when required to jump onto small areas". The remake's main fixes were to the camera and aiming ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conker%27s_Bad_Fur_Day)).

## How the humour is carried: voice first, then bodies, then sound

**The joke's rhythm was fixed in a recording before anything was built.** "For making each mission scenario, voice recording came first"; Seavor and Beanland describe "having some fun with stupid voices", and the scenes were then staged and animated to the audio. Everyone "worked in a liberated, non-planned, and intuition-based manner", with ideas passed by word of mouth and kept on notepads ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conker%27s_Bad_Fur_Day)).

**The animators were given time for small things.** "When it came to animation, small details were a priority, such as the Fire Imps reacting if a swear word was entered into the cheat code menu and the camera shaking and triggering a sound effect if hit by an object." "Conker has 2,000 animations, including 15 idle animations." "Some animations, such as drunk character movements, required research", and an animator was taught to juggle so that Conker could. Props in his idles were pinned to whichever joint made them move best: "Conker's juggling balls moved based on his right hip, the Game Boy was attached to his foot". The price was speed: by the estimate of the composer and one of the animators, "8.7 seconds and two-to-three cycles of animation were completed per day".

**The jokes sit where the player cannot change the pace.** "The length of all cutscenes combined total around two hours." A critic quoted on Game Developer, with Conker among his examples, explains why comedy games do this: "It's easier to control the comedic timing when the player can't control the pace" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/comedic-sound-for-the-game-music-composer)). A comedy writer in another Game Developer feature gives the other side: in play, the player "can hear your punch line before the set-up. He can skip the set-up of a joke altogether" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/no-laughing-matter-making-humor-work-in-games)).

**The tool had one flaw, and a later studio's did not.** Marlow built a cutscene editor that put together "separately-made animations, audio files, visual effects, and camera setups". But scenes "had to be played in their entirety before they could be altered again", which made small changes, "adjustments to the timing of speech bubbles to match camera angle changes" among them, "very tedious". Four years later Double Fine described theirs for Psychonauts: "a visual timeline", "rows of 'actors' for each scene", where "you could place an action at a specific time to play animations, place and orient a camera or actor", and "the cutscene could be previewed at any time". It "put control of the cinematography in the hands of the animators" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/classic-postmortem-double-fine-s-i-psychonauts-i-)).

**Sound was chosen for laughs.** The frying pan replaced a baseball bat "because it justified the use of a comic sound effect of a metal object hitting something". Reviewers praised music with "arrangement variation based on player location" (done by raising and lowering separate tracks) and "Conker's footsteps changing sound effects step-by-step". The game won the 2001 BAFTA for sound. The voices filled most of the cartridge: "Audio alone occupied roughly 40–48 MB" of 64.

### What a silent hero changes

All of that begins with a voice, and the knight has none. The sources below are not about Conker; they are the nearest things read to what RATQUEST has to do instead.

**The quiet one is the straight man.** Thank Goodness You're Here! (2024), made by two people, is Conker's formula with the pads reduced to one slap and the hero reduced to a foil for everyone else. (Whether its hero ever speaks was not checked; the interview does not say.) Its makers: "Our approach to development is comedy first, and then tease out 'gameplay' from those bits"; "the player character serves as a kind of straight man for the comedy"; "Slap was the simplest and funniest" way to touch the world. They call the result "functionally a sketch show you can play", and name the same two troubles Conker's reviewers did. Signposting: "it was tough to signal to the player who and what is important in a scene at any given moment". And the player's freedom: he "can walk away from the sketches and decide instead to slap a bin basically whenever they want". It also ran "3 years over schedule" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-comedy-first-approach-to-thank-goodness-you-re-here)).

**Without words, plain actions read best.** A close reading of Sonic 3 & Knuckles, whose scenes have no dialogue: "Since characters show their intentions through a pantomime, these obvious actions don't come across as insipid or silly. He looks around, closes the door and runs away" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/sonic-3-knuckles-design-lessons-in-transition-travel-and-pantomime)). Look, then act; one intention at a time.

**A few strong faces or poses are enough, and realism is not the aim.** The animator of Little Kitty, Big City found that true-to-life movement "felt a bit bland", that a rig limited to "happiness, sadness, surprise, and anger" helped more than it hurt, that one curve should run through the whole body, and that breaking the curve is itself a joke ("a jagged line as punctuation"). Her summary: "more of a pantomime of what a human expects a cat to be thinking rather than realism" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/art/deep-dive-how-little-kitty-big-city-rejects-realism-to-achieve-authenticity)).

**Someone has to have the last word.** Al Lowe, who made the Leisure Suit Larry games: "Somebody, somewhere has to have the authority to say, 'This sucks. This has gotta go.' Humor is editing." And: "Funny doesn't have to be expensive, and funny doesn't have to be huge" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/no-laughing-matter-making-humor-work-in-games)). This is what Seavor's promotion did for Conker: the cancelled version had "lots of fun ideas" and no one to say which.

## How it was made, briefly

It began in 1996 as a cute Mario 64-style platformer, was shown in 1997 as Conker's Quest and in 1998 as Twelve Tales: Conker 64, and was doubted for being one more cute platformer beside Rare's own Banjo-Kazooie. Inside, "fights between workers at the barn delayed the process", and a programmer recalled "it just really wasn't gelling as a finished game". Seavor, then an artist on it, pitched a game "about Conker trying to help others but causing more problems in doing so" and was made lead; the engine, the hero and much of the world were kept. "Development lasted four years" by Wikipedia's account; two Rare staff who were not on its team remembered "about four and a half, five years" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/a-rare-opportunity-on-pi-atas-microsoft-and-more)). About a fifth of the planned play was cut for time. It reviewed at 92 out of 100 and sold "only about 55,000 copies within its first month", an expensive game for adults at the end of a family console's life that Nintendo kept at arm's length. The 2005 Xbox remake looked better, fixed the camera, was more censored, and was faulted for "the lack of refinement in areas such as facial animation that was perfected on the Nintendo 64"; when Rare reissued its catalogue in 2015 it chose the original ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conker%27s_Bad_Fur_Day), [Live & Reloaded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conker:_Live_%26_Reloaded), [Rare Replay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rare_Replay)). Team size was not found anywhere.

The lesson in the history (our reading): the game was not rescued by a new plan or better platforming. It was rescued by one finished scene that made the bosses laugh, an instruction to "make more of that", and one person to judge every scene after.

## What this means for RATQUEST

Each suggestion names what in the game it touches, what finding it rests on, and how firm that finding is. "Read" means a page read in full here; for anything the Conker team said or did, that page is Wikipedia reporting them, never the makers themselves. "Interview" means makers' own words read on Game Developer (never the Conker team). "Second-hand" means a search summary only. "Our reading" means a conclusion drawn here. Everything stays inside the owner's standing rules: nothing below makes the game easier, nothing is a pop-up banner, the knight stays silent, mouths stay still, the talk-box portraits stay as they are, the view in play stays the player's, the story stays parked, and these are the Club's own people and jokes, not that bar's. Nothing in the game has been changed.

### The Club and its regulars

**1. Keep staging the room; do not simulate it.** The Club's method as built (a fixed spot each, a loop for what each is `doing`, pairs who take turns, heads that turn to the rat) is the method both of the owner's references used, as far as can be read. There is no finding anywhere in this research that argues for regulars who wander, choose seats or keep a timetable. Spend nothing there. **Rests on:** the pub, Windy and the cantina (read, and second-hand for the pub's inside); "staged" as the conclusion (our reading).

**2. Give every regular one small reaction to the knight before anyone gets a long scene.** This is the largest gap between the Club and what Conker's animators were told to care about. Today a busy regular turns his head to the rat, and as far as START-HERE and a look through the code show, that is all. One reaction each, about a second long, to the things a player will certainly do: jump onto the turtles' table (the thimbles hop, Mo's neck goes in); swing a sword beside the card game (Ash claps his cards to his chest); strike the keg (it rings, and Madam Tawny's head tips right over to look at him); stand on the bar. Conker's animators managed under nine seconds of animation a day, so twenty one-second reactions are a far better buy than one twenty-second scene, and a reaction is a joke the player causes, so it cannot be missed or heard out of order. Add the view's own flinch where it fits (a small shake when the keg is struck): Conker counted the camera among its reacting things. **Rests on:** "small details were a priority", the Fire Imps, the camera shake, 8.7 seconds a day (read); the comedy writer on punch lines heard before set-ups (interview).

**3. When a regular first arrives, stage it. No banner.** The room gaining Biscuit, Professor Snow, Corporal Nib or Sir Hobnail is the Club's whole sense of growth, and at present a new regular is simply there next time. Head count is the one thing Conker's bar proves players feel, and the cantina was built as an entrance. So: the first time the knight walks into the Club after a regular's `when` comes true, play a few seconds in which the newcomer comes in at the door and crosses to his spot while two or three heads turn, then hand back control. It is the natural first use of the cutscene system (below), it replaces any thought of a "NEW REGULAR" notice, and it must be skippable and play once. **Rests on:** the pub full and then empty (read); the cantina as a planned "shock" (read); that this is how to use it (our reading).

**4. Mark the one regular who has something new by how he stands, and only one at a time.** Conker's standing complaint was not knowing where to go; the makers of Thank Goodness You're Here! found the same thing hard; the cure Conker reached for was that pads "inform players of what needs to be done next". The Club now has up to fifteen talkers and no way to tell who has a new line or a pointer to a wedge. Without banners, the signal has to be a body: one `doing` kept for "I want a word" (he stands, or waves the rat over, or simply watches him across the room with his whole body turned, which no busy regular otherwise does). At most one regular does it at once. **Rests on:** pads as signposts and Next Generation's complaint (read); the signposting quote (interview).

**5. One resident, one purpose, one place.** Windy's residents each stand at their own landmark with a job or a door. Go down the Club's list and write, beside each name, the one thing he is for: Pip sells, Madam Tawny keeps the bar, Sir Hobnail minds the south door, Dave plays. Where the answer is "he has some lines", give him a purpose that points OUT of the Club and costs no new collectible: a regular who used to be an enemy knows one thing about where his kind stand guard (Sir Hobnail and the Foundry, where the Stag Knights are; Corporal Nib and the Ant Pikemen's posts), so that talking to the former enemy is how the player hears where a wedge's climb begins. That is the three-beat shape of suggestion 15, with the Club as its first beat. **Rests on:** Windy's residents and "help other characters" (read).

**6. Former enemies: keep the kind a hazard, make the individual a person with something wrong with him.** This is Conker's weasels and beetles, and the Club already does it twice. What the research adds is how such a person is written fast: one loud idea, visible in his idle. For instance: Sir Hobnail is too big for every stool and has tried; Nib comes to attention when the knight draws his sword and then pretends he did not. It also answers "no rule yet for the bosses": the kind/individual split says a boss need not come himself. One small member of the Broodmother's brood at the end of the bar is the same idea at a size that fits the door. **Rests on:** weasels, beetles, multiplayer sides (read; the beetles as a hazard second-hand); "basically had something wrong with them" (read, of Banjo-Kazooie); the brood suggestion (our reading).

**7. One comic idea per kind of creature, carried in the body since it cannot be carried in the voice.** Conker gave each group an accent. The Club has begun the wordless version without being told to: ferrets "cannot keep still", owls' heads "tip right over", a turtle's neck "comes out when he speaks". Treat that as the rule and finish the set: decide the one bodily habit of the foxes, the mice, the birds, the pigeon, the ant and the stag beetle, and make every loop, reaction and cutscene pose for that kind start from it. The voices as they are (a borrowed recording at each one's own pitch) are the Banjo-Kazooie answer and need no more than that; the "voices of their own" on START-HERE's not-done list can wait behind everything else here. **Rests on:** three voices and one vocal idea per group (read); mumble voices and pitch-shifting (read).

**8. Let sound do half the populating.** Three cheap things that Conker's reviewers singled out. Footsteps by surface: rug, plank, the dance floor's tiles. Music by place: Dave's beat full by the stage and dance floor, thinner and under a murmur by the bar and tables, as Conker raised and lowered tracks by location; and since Wren already says Dave "turns the beat up" with every wedge, make that true and audible, one more layer of the track per world cleared, which is growth the player hears from the door. Props chosen for their noise: the player will hit everything, so the keg, the row of thimbles, the bottle shelves and the bar should each ring differently under a sword or a shield. Beyond that, overheard half-lines in the regulars' mumble voices as he passes a table, with no text box, are a room talking; a writer on such lines advises treating them as "small excerpts of a longer conversation". **Rests on:** location music, footsteps, the frying pan (read); the overheard-lines advice ([Game Developer blog](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/adding-life-to-worlds-with-dialogue-barks), read in part, about other games).

**9. Put the Club behind the title, if it is not there already.** The cleverest thing reported of Conker's bar is that the player sees the full room every time the game starts, because the menu lives in it. The title screen already draws a live scene behind it (`src/ui/boot.ts`; which scene was not checked here). If that scene is the Club with the regulars at their tables, the room is familiar before the first step. **Rests on:** the pub as the menu (second-hand, from fan-wiki summaries only); it is a sound idea whether or not that is exactly how Conker did it.

### Context-sensitive moments

**10. Use them in the Club, never in the levels.** A pad does the fitting thing for the player; it has no skill in it. In a level that would mean a marked spot doing what the knight's own jump, kick, grab or guard should do, which is the game made easier and the wall jump given less to do, both against the owner's word. In the Club nothing needs skill, and a spot where one press makes him take part in the room is exactly what a silent knight among talkers is missing. **Rests on:** what pads are and Edge's "minigames" verdict (read); the conclusion (our reading).

**11. Build each Club spot to five rules.** (a) The thing itself is the pad: a free stool, the bar's edge, the dartboard, the fire. No floating marker and no banner; the small hint the game already shows for talking is enough. (b) The same button as talking, always. (c) He is never locked in: any move walks him out of it, so no moves are taken away. (d) It is quick. (e) Each kind of spot exists more than once or can be used again and again (every free stool can be sat on), so none is a one-off. And each should end on a small pay-off from the room, because that is where the laugh is: he sits at the turtles' table and both stop talking and look at him until he leaves; he raps the bar and Madam Tawny slides a thimble along it, which is the owner's own scene ("sliding a glass down the bar") turned into something the player does; he throws at the dartboard and the foxes watch the dart, not their cards. **Rests on:** the three rules and "once is too rare", "never be contextually useless", "not locked out of your other options" (blog essays and one critic, read in full, none about Conker); the Alka-Seltzer pad as tutorial and gag in one (read).

**12. Let an empty seat be the signpost.** The free stool beside the regular who has something new (suggestion 4) is a pad that "inform[s] players of what needs to be done next" without a word on the screen. **Rests on:** that sentence (read).

### Humour carried by animation, with a hero who is silent

**13. The knight is the straight man.** He does ordinary knightly things (stands, walks, sits, draws, guards) and the room is funny around him. Give him a handful of plain reactions for scenes and spots, in the spirit of the four-expression cat: a start, a slow look, a shrug of the shield, a proud chest. He does not mug, wink or do bits; his squeaks stay what they are. A few Club-only idles (warming his paws at a fire bowl, leaning on the bar when he stops beside it) make him one of the crowd in the only moments he is free to act by himself. **Rests on:** "a kind of straight man for the comedy" (interview); the four expressions (interview with an animator); Conker's fifteen idles (read).

**14. Rhythm first, pose second.** Conker fixed each joke's timing in a recording and animated to it. With no recording, fix it in sound and silence instead: for any scene or reaction, lay down first when each noise happens (the mumble, the thimble set down, the pause, the scrape of the stool), listen to it with the picture off, and only then key the bodies to it. The pause before the pay-off is the thing most worth getting right, and it is free. **Rests on:** "voice recording came first" (read); the method carried over to a silent cast (our reading).

**15. Three beats, ending on a pay-off, in everything that has a character in it.** Set-up, the player's or a character's one action, pay-off. For Club spots and scenes this is suggestion 11. For levels it is the lightest useful thing to take from Conker: where a wedge involves a character at all, someone shows a want (in the Club, or at the spot), the knight does one thing with his normal moves, and something visibly changes for that someone afterwards. Seavor's reason is the right one: an act "is far more engaging if there's a motivation behind it". It does not add text, a collectible or a mechanic. **Rests on:** the formula and the motivation quote (read).

**16. Show intent plainly: look, then act, then hold.** Wordless scenes read when each character does one obvious thing at a time and the others wait. In marionette terms: turn the head to the thing, a beat, then move; when a pose lands, hold it long enough to be seen at the game's small size; never have two characters start moving in the same instant unless the collision is the joke. Break a character's usual line on purpose for the punch: the turtle wholly inside his shell, the owl's head upside down, the ferret gone rigid for once. With creatures built from "a few shaped pieces", a changed silhouette is the strongest effect on offer. **Rests on:** the pantomime reading and the animator's line of action (blog and interview, read; neither about Conker).

**17. Build one scene to finished and let the owner be the editor.** Conker's turning point was one scene that made the people in charge laugh, and "make more of that". The owner has already described his: one regular orders another a drink, and the glass slides down the bar. Build that one all the way (sound, pauses, a pay-off for where the glass ends up, the view's pan) before starting a second, show it to him, and treat his reaction as the measure for every vignette after. "Humor is editing", and on this project the editor is the owner. **Rests on:** the beehive and "make more of that" (read); Lowe (interview).

**18. Leave the crudeness and the parodies where they are.** Nothing in the owner's notes asks for them; what he named is the feel of the room. one of Conker's own reviewers thought "the shock effect would go away within an hour", the tone narrowed who would buy it, and its parodies were a way for a writer-led team to brief itself, not a reason the game is loved for its bar. **Rests on:** reception and sales (read).

### The little cutscene system

A first file for it, `src/cutscene/cutscene.ts`, was in the working folder on 2026-11-16 (START-HERE still says "not started"). It was read, not run. It treats a scene as data: a cast, props and a view, each with keys in time, sounds at their moments, and a function that says where everything is at any instant. The suggestions below are written with that in view.

**19. Keep "any frame, any time" above every other feature.** This is the one thing Conker's editor lacked, at a cost its makers called "very tedious", and the thing Psychonauts' editor had. A scene must be playable from any second, steppable a frame at a time, and viewable for one character alone, from the dev tools. The file's design already allows it; hold to it as scenes, props and sounds are added, and never let a scene depend on having been played from the start. **Rests on:** Conker's editor (read) and Psychonauts' (postmortem by its makers, read).

**20. Scenes are short, skippable, and never the only way to learn something.** Conker's two hours of cutscene are the cost not to pay, in a game whose pull is movement. A Club vignette should be seconds, not minutes; one button ends it at once; and nothing the player needs is said only inside one. A scene hung on an event the player may never cause needs a second trigger: Conker's team learned this when a tester reached a late level without ever dying, and the scene that explains dying had to be forced to play there. **Rests on:** "around two hours" and the forced death scene (read).

**21. Cut on the beat, and change the words on the cut.** Conker's hand-tuning was "the timing of speech bubbles to match camera angle changes". If a scene uses the talk box, a new line should arrive with a change of view or of who is moving, not drift across one. The owner's pan round the soldier's table is the opposite case, one long move with no words, and should be left unbroken. The composed, far, fixed views that hurt Conker in play are right at home here, because nobody is jumping. **Rests on:** speech-bubble timing and the camera complaints (read).

**22. Use it first for the small things in this list.** Arrivals (3), the pay-offs of Club spots (11), and the one finished scene (17) are all a few seconds of marionette work each, and between them they would exercise walking in, sitting, a prop moving along the bar, heads turning together, and a pan. That is a better test of the tool than a long scene, and each one ships as part of the Club. Expect to drop some: Conker shipped with about a fifth of its planned play cut. **Rests on:** the animation rate and the cuts (read); the order of work (our reading).

### Level structure

**23. Do not take Conker's road.** One fixed path of one-off scenarios is what drew "minigames", "trial and error" and "lack of clear direction". RATQUEST's worlds are open halls with wedges earned by movement, and the three earlier reports all argue for making that more so. A late change of pace built from the knight's own moves (the Long Drop's shield ride) is a different thing from Conker turning into a shooter, and is fine. **Rests on:** the reviews as quoted (read).

**24. Finish the collectibles rework; the history backs it.** The studio that made Conker had just been told "more things", was called "an interactive egg hunt" for it, and its next platformer collected one thing only and was praised for that. The Cistern is done to the owner's rule (eight things where there were thirty-two); the Foundry, the Hollows and the Keep, as START-HERE has it, still have their crumbs. **Rests on:** Donkey Kong 64 and IGN's praise (read).

**25. Decide what cheese is for once the shop is bought out.** By second-hand report Conker stops giving money once the last gate is paid, so the currency never sits useless. When Pip has nothing left that the player wants, cheese should still buy something small and visible in the Club that is not gear and is never equipped: a round for a table (and the table's reaction), a tune from Dave. **Rests on:** the money stopping (second-hand); the idea (our reading).

**26. Change the Club itself a little as the worlds fall, not only who is in it.** Windy changes by things happening to the place: a river drained, and by second-hand report a fall of night. The Club's equivalents are cheap and already half-built: the strings of lights, the dance floor's tiles that "do not flash to the beat" yet, the fire bowls, Dave's music. One visible change to the room per world cleared (more bulbs lit, the floor coming alive, a warmer light) sits on top of a room that is already full, which is the owner's rule exactly. **Rests on:** the drained river (read); night (second-hand).

**27. Keep the camera the player's.** Conker's team chose a far, fixed view to "make the game look cinematic" and it became the most repeated complaint, worst on small landings. The owner's rule that the view never turns by itself in play needs no change; this is one more game that shows why. **Rests on:** the camera choice and the reviews (read).

## Conclusion

The owner asked what makes Conker's bar feel full, and the honest answer has two halves. The half that can be stood behind: nothing read about that game, or about the cantina he named beside it, describes a room that runs itself. Both are a set of contrasting types, each given one thing to do, a fixed centre, a few faces seen close, and sound; and Conker's team put their extra care into small reactions and into scenes that end on a pay-off. The Club already has the first part of that and is missing the second, so the next work there is reactions, staged arrivals, a handful of spots where one press lets the knight join in, and one short scene finished to the owner's laugh, in that order, with the cutscene system built by making them. The half that cannot be stood behind: nobody here has seen the bar. Its inside, its menu and its drinkers are known from summaries of fan pages, and the makers' own accounts (Seavor's interviews, the director's commentary) sit on sites this environment refuses. If those can be allowed, the things most worth looking for are what each drinker in the opening is doing, shot by shot, and anything Seavor has said about staging and timing. They would sharpen the picture; given how consistent the readable evidence is, they are unlikely to turn it over. What no source can settle is whether a given reaction or scene is funny. That needs the owner in front of the build.
