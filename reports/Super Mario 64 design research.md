# Mario 64 makes every big jump earned

Super Mario 64 was built from the hero's movement outward: Nintendo spent about half the project making Mario feel good to move in a bare room of blocks, and only then built the fifteen courses, quickly, "in a single burst" ([Shmuplations, 1996 interviews](https://shmuplations.com/mario64/)). Its game code, which can be read in full, shows the result in numbers: **every one of Mario's high jumps has to be earned**, by running fast, by landing and jumping again within a sixth of a second, by crouching or turning first, or by using a wall ([decompiled source, mario.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/n64decomp/sm64/master/src/game/mario.c)). Its courses are small "dioramas" with one readable centre, each holding seven stars, of which the most criticised is the one that asks you to sweep up 100 coins. For RATQUEST this matters directly. The owner's two complaints, that he "can just jump over stuff" and that the collectibles feel like Pac-Man, line up with the two places this game differs most from Mario 64: our knight jumps **far higher for his size, anywhere, with nothing to earn it**, and several of our levels still have the crumb-trail and "every crumb" goals that Mario 64's own critics single out. The last section turns this into specific changes, each tied to what the game already has and marked by how firmly it is sourced. One caution runs through everything: the strongest evidence is the game's code and the developers' 1996 interviews; much else (fan wikis, Iwata Asks, design videos) could not be read because this environment's network refused those sites.

## What could be read, and how much to trust it

The research had three kinds of source, and this report keeps them apart. **First-hand and solid**: the game's decompiled source code on GitHub, a byte-for-byte rebuild of the shipped game, which gives exact jump speeds, gravity, timing windows and camera distances ([n64decomp/sm64](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/n64decomp/sm64/master/src/game/mario.c)); and two 1996 Japanese strategy-guide interviews with the team (Shigeru Miyamoto alone, then a roundtable with Takashi Tezuka, Yoshiaki Koizumi and three programmers), read in full in a fan translation ([Shmuplations](https://shmuplations.com/mario64/)). Two more interviews with Miyamoto were read in part ([Miyamoto and Eno, 1996](https://shmuplations.com/miyamotoxeno/); [Miyamoto and Nagoshi, 1999](https://shmuplations.com/miyamotoxnagoshi/)). **Reported, read in full**: a journalist's write-up of Koizumi's 2007 conference talk ([Game Developer, MIGS 2007](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/migs-2007-nintendo-s-koizumi-on-the-path-from-garden-to-i-galaxy-i-)), and Wikipedia's article, whose own underlying sources were not checked ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Mario_64)). **Opinion**: design essays on Game Developer and a critic's blog, useful as views, not facts.

**What could not be read at all**: Iwata Asks (Nintendo's own interview series), MarioWiki, Ukikipedia (the technical wiki), The Cutting Room Floor, Nintendo Life, the Source Gaming interviews with programmer Giles Goddard, Game Maker's Toolkit, GDC talks, AV Club, a 2024 academic paper that measured Mario's speeds from video, Goomba Stomp's course-by-course reviews, and the full text of Steve Swink's "Game Feel" feature. Anything that would have come only from those is left out, or marked unverified where a search summary hinted at it. (`research_notes/README.md` lists what was read and what was not, for all three research projects.)

## Two years, a room of blocks, and courses made last

**Production ran from September 1994 to May 1996 with a team of "around fifteen to twenty people"**, according to Wikipedia, which also says it began on a powerful Silicon Graphics computer because the Nintendo 64 did not exist yet ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Mario_64)). Miyamoto confirms that last part himself: "we actually did our work on a large, powerful computer that simulated what we guessed the N64 hardware specs would be", and when the real prototype arrived and "handled the movement and controls almost perfectly", the team "quickly dashed off a planning spec sheet" ([Shmuplations](https://shmuplations.com/mario64/)).

The start was "deceptively simple": "a room made of simple lego-like blocks, and Mario and Luigi could run around in there, climb slopes, jump around." The aim was to get the analogue stick right, "and once that felt smooth, we knew we were halfway there." Miyamoto's stated method is "we get the fundamentals solid first, then do as much with that core concept as our time and ambition will allow", and he puts a number on it: **"We spent about half our time and energy designing the basic system... As for the courses and enemies, those actually came at the very end. They were done in a single burst of energy, just thrown together, almost"** ([Shmuplations](https://shmuplations.com/mario64/)). Wikipedia adds the story of a golden rabbit called MIPS as the first test of controls and physics, later kept in the game as a star holder; that detail is second-hand only ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Mario_64)).

Courses had **no blueprints**: "only some concept art sketches, and brief notes/memos." Miyamoto talked ideas through with course director Yoichi Yamada, who sketched them, and the designers built straight into the game. Miyamoto compares it to "sculpting a diorama out of clay. First you make a very general shape." His example is the first course, Bob-omb Battlefield: it began with a river to cross, but when they dropped Mario in, "the river flows too fast and it sweeps him away", so they swapped it for a dry valley. Tezuka adds that leftover shapes with no purpose were simply given one ("let's just make it a little doghouse"). The team also, unusually for them, **watched about ten middle-schoolers play the King Bob-omb stage for half a day**; one child tried "dozens of times" to climb a hill that could not be climbed, and the children still said they wanted to play again. Miyamoto's conclusion: "if a game was this fun to play even if you weren't getting anywhere, well, it must be alright" ([Shmuplations](https://shmuplations.com/mario64/)).

A great deal was cut. **Luigi was in the game "until February" 1996 and was removed "due to memory issues"**; a Yoshi event was dropped because they "weren't satisfied with how it came out"; Mario was once able to throw the rabbit; monkeys appeared in more places; and the enemy roster was "just under 40" where they would "normally" have about 80 ([Shmuplations](https://shmuplations.com/mario64/)). Wikipedia reports that the November 1995 trade-show build had 32 courses and Miyamoto hoped for up to 40 before settling on 15, though that figure rests on sources not checked here, and a "course" then may not mean the same as a final course ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Mario_64)). Looking back in 1996, Miyamoto regretted one thing in particular: that "it became a matter of wanting to jam in every single idea that seemed interesting", leaving "less room for free, open-ended play" ([Miyamoto and Eno](https://shmuplations.com/miyamotoxeno/)).

## A slow-starting, forgiving body that hides its height behind skill

The developers' own words describe a deliberate philosophy. **"We wanted to make a game where just moving Mario around was fun"** ([Shmuplations](https://shmuplations.com/mario64/)). They chose slow acceleration knowingly: at the 1995 show people said "the controls feel really wobbly and slippery", and Miyamoto says "we weren't about to back down", because "we really did want to change the culture of gaming... that's reflected in the controls, and how long it takes to accelerate." The physics is tuned, not real: programmer Yasunari Nishida says "if you rely on the actual laws of physics too much it doesn't really make for a good game. The floatiness of Mario's jump... works great in a game", and Miyamoto adds that each kind of jump needed "different calculations." Mario's weight comes from hand-made animation centred on the hips (motion capture was tried and dropped), and Miyamoto is cheerful about the cheating: "With 3D, little 'lies' like that can go unnoticed. So we lied a lot!" The team made **193 animations, with about 50 more thrown out**, because "as players got good at the controls, they'd want to try out more and more button combinations" ([Shmuplations](https://shmuplations.com/mario64/)). A hold-to-dash button was tried and removed, partly out of "guilt" at making small children press two buttons at once ([Miyamoto and Eno](https://shmuplations.com/miyamotoxeno/)).

The most important single design change came late. In 2D Mario games "we were able to measure the number of pixels Mario could jump", but in 3D **"we had to design the levels so that as long as your jump was 'close enough', you'd make it; it was too hard for the player to judge. This was a design change we made in the middle of the development... There was a lot of booing from the staff"** ([Shmuplations](https://shmuplations.com/mario64/)). Koizumi's later talk names the other tools that went with it: a shadow dropped straight under Mario and under "every floating object", because "it's much easier to play with the shadow directly below", and "large landmarks so players could orient themselves without stopping" (the landmark remark is about Mario games in general, not Mario 64 alone) ([Game Developer, MIGS 2007](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/migs-2007-nintendo-s-koizumi-on-the-path-from-garden-to-i-galaxy-i-)).

The code shows how the move set rewards skill without punishing beginners. The table below puts Mario's numbers, worked out from the source, beside our knight's, both measured in the hero's own body heights (Mario is 160 game units tall; the knight 1.1). The Mario figures are simple arithmetic on the code's numbers and ignore small effects, so treat them as close, not exact.

| | Super Mario 64 | RATQUEST knight |
|---|---|---|
| Top running speed | 6.0 body heights a second | 5.6 |
| One jump, standing | 1.4 high | 2.3 |
| One jump at a full run | 1.95 high (faster run, higher jump) | 2.3 (speed makes no difference) |
| Second jump | 2.8 high, but only by jumping again within 0.17 s of landing | 4.3 in all, a jump in mid-air, any time, anywhere |
| Third jump | 3.7 high, only from a run above two thirds of top speed | none |
| Backflip, side flip | 3.0 high, from a crouch or a sharp turn | none |
| Wall kick | 3.0 high, **higher than his second jump** | 2.5, **well under his double jump** |
| Window to kick off a wall | 0.17 s | 0.16 s |
| Grace after running off an edge | none found in the code read | 0.1 s |

Sources: Mario from the decompiled code ([mario.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/n64decomp/sm64/master/src/game/mario.c), [mario_actions_moving.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/n64decomp/sm64/master/src/game/mario_actions_moving.c), [mario_actions_airborne.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/n64decomp/sm64/master/src/game/mario_actions_airborne.c), [mario_step.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/n64decomp/sm64/master/src/game/mario_step.c)); the knight from `src/data/tuning.ts`. The wall kick's height has one wrinkle: one line in the source sets a lower speed (giving 2.1 high) that the researcher reads as being overwritten a moment later by the higher one; that reading was not cross-checked against Ukikipedia.

What this shows is the heart of the matter for this game. **In Mario 64, speed is something you carry from move to move**: the third jump needs a fast run, the long jump needs speed from a crouch, the dive needs speed from a jump, and at low speed each simply turns into a plain jump or punch ([mario_actions_moving.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/n64decomp/sm64/master/src/game/mario_actions_moving.c)). A beginner can still get round; a skilled player goes higher and further. And **a wall is where a single move gives Mario the most height for the least set-up**: one kick, timed within a sixth of a second of touching the wall, beats his second jump. No developer is quoted saying "we made high jumps earned" or "we made the wall kick the reward"; that is our reading of the numbers. But the numbers themselves are certain.

## Fifteen dioramas, one landmark each, seven stars apiece

Mario 64's structure is verified both from the code and from Wikipedia. Peach's Castle is a hub with paintings as doors; there are **15 courses with seven stars each (six named missions plus one for 100 coins), and 15 hidden stars, for 120** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Mario_64)). Star doors in the code open at **8, 30, 50 and 70 stars** (doors for 1 and 3 exist as models but their placement was not found), and the final stage needs 70, so **50 stars, about two fifths, are optional** ([castle_inside/script.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/n64decomp/sm64/master/levels/castle_inside/script.c)). Miyamoto says the castle idea "was originally something I thought of for Zelda" ([Shmuplations](https://shmuplations.com/mario64/)).

The exact star names, read from the game's own text, show how each course works ([text/us/courses.h](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/n64decomp/sm64/master/text/us/courses.h)). The first star usually names the course's landmark ("Big Bob-omb on the Summit", "To the Top of the Fortress", "The Big House in the Sky"). **Every course has a star for eight red coins.** Several have a "reach the place you cannot reach" star by cannon or wing cap ("Shoot to the Island in the Sky", "Blast to the Lonely Mushroom"). And one is named for a move: **"Wall Kicks Will Work"** in Cool, Cool Mountain (whether that star actually requires wall kicks could not be checked). The pattern, our reading, is one readable centre with missions as different routes to or from it, in one shared space, so the player learns a course's "grammar" fast. Game Developer's Nathan Savant puts it as a puzzle box: "Stars play the role of the articulation points you find on a puzzle box... rather than exploring a natural environment, Mario was running around on a toy" ([Game Developer, Mario 64 v Odyssey](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/mario-64-v-odyssey)). He also charts the game's difficulty: it "starts off being primarily about exploration... and ends primarily being about testing advanced skills."

Variety came from three places. Each course is a different place to move in (a mountain, a fortress, a sunken ship, a clock), and each is "sculpted" until movement feels right in it. The same course is reused for six different goals. And **the bosses are built from movement, not from a separate fight system**: the code has King Bob-omb hurt only by being grabbed and thrown onto his hill, King Whomp only by a ground pound after he flops down, and Bowser by being swung round with the stick and thrown at a mine, each three hits or fewer ([king_bobomb.inc.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/n64decomp/sm64/master/src/game/behaviors/king_bobomb.inc.c), [whomp.inc.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/n64decomp/sm64/master/src/game/behaviors/whomp.inc.c), [bowser.inc.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/n64decomp/sm64/master/src/game/behaviors/bowser.inc.c)). Miyamoto's stated aim for the whole is openness: "I want a game that allows players to try come up with their own solutions", and the castle grounds were made for the half of players who "would hang out and explore outside" ([Shmuplations](https://shmuplations.com/mario64/)).

The weak spot, by the critics' account, is the hunting. Celia Wagar, who rates the level design highly and praises that there is "almost never only one path forwards", says: "I don't like the 100 coin challenges. I don't like the scavenging for the stars" ([CritPoints](https://critpoints.net/2015/09/30/mario-galaxy-relative-to-sunshine64/)). Goomba Stomp's reviews reportedly call Tall, Tall Mountain's 100-coin star "incredibly tedious" and complain of backtracking in Hazy Maze Cave's, but those pages were not read, only search summaries of them. Later games went the other way: Odyssey's moons are "not the destination, but objects along the road", the first one about fifty feet from the start ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/mario-64-v-odyssey)). **The owner's wish (few things, each a destination) is Mario 64's puzzle box, not Odyssey's roadside.**

## The camera explained itself, and players still called it chaotic

Koizumi recalls Miyamoto saying that "the greatest appeal of working on 3d games is the camera"; the team weighed a side-on camera, a tower camera and a follow camera, and Koizumi's line is **"Thinking about the camera is game design, too"** ([Game Developer, MIGS 2007](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/migs-2007-nintendo-s-koizumi-on-the-path-from-garden-to-i-galaxy-i-)). The camera is shown as Lakitu, a character filming Mario. In the code it has many modes, some following Mario and some set by hand for particular places (fixed, radial, spiral stairs, boss fights), with the C-buttons turning it in 90-degree steps, zooming out, or looking up in first person. Its default distance is about 800 game units, roughly **five Mario heights**, and it swings itself round slowly towards its target ([camera.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/n64decomp/sm64/master/src/game/camera.c); only the first quarter of that file was read).

It was not loved. Miyamoto said in 1996: "we get a lot of users saying it moves around chaotically on its own and makes the game hard to play" ([Miyamoto and Eno](https://shmuplations.com/miyamotoxeno/)). Wikipedia lists reviewers docking points for "erratic movements" and for not reaching the angle they wanted ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Mario_64)). Koizumi later said that giving players free camera control (in Sunshine and Ocarina of Time) made "the overall control scheme... too complex" and "divided the group of people that might have enjoyed the game", and Galaxy's aim became to "play without ever having to think about the camera" ([Game Developer, MIGS 2007](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/migs-2007-nintendo-s-koizumi-on-the-path-from-garden-to-i-galaxy-i-)). The lesson, as read here: the best camera help is one the level designer places on purpose for a particular spot, not one that turns by itself everywhere.

## What this means for RATQUEST

Each suggestion below names what in the game it touches, which finding it rests on, and how well that finding is sourced. "Code" means Mario 64's own source, first-hand; "interview" means the developers' words in the 1996 interviews, first-hand in translation; "talk" means Koizumi's 2007 talk as reported; "secondary" means Wikipedia; "opinion" means a critic; "our reading" means a conclusion drawn here, not stated by anyone at Nintendo. Nothing in the game has been changed yet.

### Make the wall kick the knight's best way up, as Mario's is

Today the knight's double jump climbs about 4.75 units (4.3 body heights) from flat ground with nothing asked of the player, while one wall kick gives only about 2.8 (2.5 body heights). In Mario 64 it is the other way round: one wall kick (3.0) beats the second jump (2.8). That is part of why the owner keeps finding he can jump over things, and why the wall jump feels optional. Two ways to fix it, to try on the Tweaks sliders before anything is committed: raise `wallKickUp` (now 10) so that one kick climbs more than the double jump's second half, or lower `doubleVelocity` (now 9) so the double jump is a smaller boost. The first leaves existing routes working and makes chimneys climb faster (the 2.2-wide chimney's 1.8 a kick would rise); the second makes the game harder everywhere and breaks routes that are written around a full double jump (the Faith Stones, the High Hollow's mushrooms, the Slag Run, the Cistern's Outrigger), so it would mean re-running `reach.ts` and every level test. **Rests on**: the jump and wall-kick heights (code, solid; the one wall-kick wrinkle noted above); the idea that Mario's height is earned (our reading).

### Tie extra height to doing something, not just pressing jump twice

None of Mario's high jumps is free; ours is. Short of removing the air jump, there are milder steps that keep the game's feel: make the first jump a little higher at a full run than standing (Mario's rises from 1.4 to 1.95 body heights with speed), so a run-up matters; or let the air jump come back only after a wall kick or a ledge, so that the big heights come from walls. Either changes what every level allows, so `reach.ts` and the short-cut checks must be run after. This one is for the owner to feel by hand, which nobody has done yet with the current numbers. **Rests on**: speed-dependent jump height and the second jump's 0.17-second landing window (code, solid); "earned" as the design intent (our reading, no developer quote).

### Give every world at least two wall-kick climbs, and a wedge named for one

Mario 64 names a whole star "Wall Kicks Will Work", and Swink's point (read only in a summary) is that "without a wall, there can be no wall kick... constraints define sensation" ([Game Developer summary of Swink](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/feature-game-feel---the-secret-ingredient-)). The Cistern now has three kick climbs and the Keep has one ("Up the Chimney"); the Foundry and the Hollows have none (START-HERE counted two chimneys in the whole game before the Cistern was reworked, and nothing in the Foundry's or the Hollows' level files mentions one). Natural places: the Lift Shaft is already a vertical shaft whose wedge is "Lifts, pillars, another lift, then all the way along the planks", and a chimney to its top would make it a climb rather than a ride; the High Hollow's last step into the nook in the north wall could be a chimney instead of one more mushroom. Use the Cistern's measured rules (2.2 wide gains about 1.8 a kick, 4 wide about 2.7, a landing at least 2.4 thick, five or more from any other wall). **Rests on**: the star name (code text, solid); Swink (secondary summary, not read in full); whether that star requires kicks (unverified).

### Replace the "every crumb" goals with one earned prize each

"Every Crumb in the Shaft" ("Cheese on every landing, all the way up") and "Every Crumb in the Webs" are our 100-coin stars: the one Mario 64 mission that is purely a sweep, and the one its critics single out. The Foundry's and the Keep's "Eight Bottle Caps" have hints like "in a crate", "under the gantry", "in the corner", which is scavenging, not movement. Do these as the Cistern was done: trade crumbs for a few hunks of cheese worth the same in all, put each cap at the top of a piece of movement, and turn each "every crumb" wedge into a single route wedge (the Webs' could be a high ledge reached wall to wall). Mario 64's red coins are a useful model for the count: **a fixed, small number (eight) per course**, enough to tour a level without a trail of them. **Rests on**: the course structure and red-coin count (code and secondary, solid); the critics' dislike of the 100-coin hunt (opinion, one read source, the rest unverified).

### Hang each prize on a landmark the player can see from afar

Mario's first star in each course names the course's centre, and Koizumi says large landmarks let players find their way without stopping. Our halls have such centres already: the Cistern's tower, the Foundry's Crucible, the Hollows' Spider Rock, the Keep's battlements. Aim for each hall's few prizes to sit on or around a landmark visible from the entrance. One catch is specific to this game: the camera sees only about 5 to 7 units up a wall, so a cap at the top of a ten-high chimney is invisible from its foot. The game already drops a shadow under floating collectibles (`main.ts`), which is exactly Koizumi's "reference point on the ground"; make sure the chimney's prize casts one at its foot, or give it a glint seen from across the hall, so the player knows there is something up there. **Rests on**: star names (code text, solid); landmarks and shadows (talk, reported, read in full; landmarks said of Mario games generally).

### Keep the hall open, and gate only the last step

Mario 64's courses are deliberately open, with "almost never only one path forwards"; the owner wants one intended way to each prize. These fit together if the halls stay open to roam and the final few units to each prize are the part that only the intended move can do. The Cistern's method already does this: play the route, then take its key piece away and ask `unreachable()` whether the prize can still be got. Do that for every prize in the Foundry, Hollows and Keep, remembering the heights to beat: about 4.75 for a double jump and 5.8 with a grab. **Rests on**: Miyamoto on open play (interview, solid); the "one path" observation (opinion).

### Make landings fair and the getting there hard

Miyamoto changed Mario 64 mid-development so that a jump "close enough" would land, because depth on a TV is "too hard for the player to judge." The Leap of Faith's wedge is "Six leaps, each at the very edge of a running jump", which is the opposite. The owner does not want the game easier, and this need not make it so: keep the difficulty in timing, moving platforms and chained moves (the Winch, the sliders, a wall kick at the right moment), but give landings a little more room than the strict maximum, so a player fails because he mistimed, not because he misjudged depth. The jump checker could flag any required jump within a small margin of the knight's limit, so it is a choice and not an accident. **Rests on**: the "close enough" change (interview, solid; said of players new to 3D in 1996).

### Build a move garden before reworking the Foundry

Mario's team proved movement in a room of blocks before building courses. The level editor makes this cheap here: one test room with a 2.2 and a 4-wide chimney, wall-to-wall gaps, and ledges at 4.5, 4.75, 5.8 and 6, so the owner can feel on his phone which heights need which move, and so any change to the jump numbers above can be judged in one place before the three remaining worlds are rebuilt to it. **Rests on**: the block-room start and "half our time... on the basic system" (interview, solid).

### Let the camera help on wall-kick routes, placed by hand, never turning itself

The owner's decision that the view must not swing round by itself has strong backing: Mario's camera did turn itself, and Miyamoto admits players called it "chaotic"; Koizumi's later aim was a camera nobody has to think about. Mario's real answer was cameras set by hand for awkward places. The knight's view sits 3.5 units back (about 3.2 body heights) against Lakitu's roughly five Mario heights, and sees only 5 to 7 units up a wall. A camera zone placed on each chimney that pulls the view back and tilts it up the shaft (height and tilt only, never a turn, in keeping with the 2026-11-15 rule for low obstacles) would show the climb's top. **Rests on**: the camera complaint (interview, solid); hand-set camera modes and the default distance (code, solid, though only a quarter of the camera file was read).

### Keep the doors' shares where they are

Mario 64 lets the player finish with 70 of 120 stars (58 per cent) and leaves 50 optional; our Keep door asks 25 of 47 wedges (53 per cent), a close match. If "every crumb" wedges are turned into route wedges rather than removed, the count does not change and `tests/balance.ts` stays happy; if any are removed, the doors need looking at, as that test will say. **Rests on**: the door thresholds (code, solid) and the 120 total (secondary, consistent with the code's star names).

## Conclusion

The most useful thing in this research is not a quote but a pattern in the numbers: Mario 64's height is always paid for, and the wall is where it pays best. RATQUEST's knight has the reverse, a free double jump that out-climbs his wall kick, which goes a long way to explain why he can jump over things, and why the wall jump has been left with so little to do. The levels can be patched wall by wall, as the Cistern was, but a game whose best move is "press jump twice" will keep asking for taller walls; making the wall kick the best way up turns the owner's favourite mechanic into the one the levels are built around. What remains unknown is how it feels in the hand, which no source can settle: that needs the move garden, the sliders and the owner's own thumb. The pages that could not be read (Iwata Asks above all, and the technical wikis) might add the developers' reasons for individual courses and the wall kick, and are worth fetching if the network allows, but they would sharpen this picture rather than change it.
