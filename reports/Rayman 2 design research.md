# Rayman 2 puts one ride in each level and never changes the buttons

Rayman 2: The Great Escape (Ubi Soft, 1999; the owner named the Nintendo 64 version) is a string of about twenty mostly linear levels, and its signature is that nearly every one has a short stretch where the hero moves **some other way**: he slides down a wet chute, rides a walking rocket he has just tired out, water-skis behind a snake, spins a chair down a track, runs along a boardwalk with a warship behind him. Its director said the team "tried to imagine some unique game sequences, like being chased by a pirate spaceship" ([Game Developer, 2002 book excerpt](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/game-design-secrets-of-the-sages----creating-characters-storyboarding-and-design-documents)). On the evidence that could be gathered, those rides share four habits: **each is one part of a level and not a level; each is met in a small, safe form before it is a test; each keeps the ordinary buttons (jump still jumps, shoot still shoots); and the camera is set by the level's designers while it lasts.** For RATQUEST this bears most on the Long Drop, which already asks more of one ride than Rayman 2 ever did (ninety-one seconds without leaving the shield, against stretches that seem to last well under a minute each), and which cannot borrow Rayman 2's camera answer because here the view must never turn by itself. So the useful lessons are about **shape**: what to teach before the ride, how to cut it into acts, when to put something behind the rider, what the ride should hand to the boss at its foot. Two things to leave alone: Rayman's hover (the knight's jump set is settled, and a glide would undo it) and his thousand small pickups (the owner wants fewer). One caution runs through everything: **the ride-by-ride account of Rayman 2 in this report is almost all second-hand**, because the pages that describe the levels, and the one article in which a level designer of the game speaks at length, could not be opened from here.

## Six people in 2D, then seventy in 3D

The game began in early 1996 as a side-scrolling sequel: "a team of six people" and ten million francs, with Rayman able "to move between foreground and background sections". Then, "with the rise of 3D games, the team changed direction and turned it into a 3D platformer inspired by Super Mario 64", and "70-80 developers, many of whom inexperienced, ultimately worked on Rayman 2 for more than two years" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayman_2:_The_Great_Escape)). Ancel directed, his first time doing so, and was also lead designer and art director ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Ancel)). His Montpellier studio had room for about thirty people, so the rest were elsewhere; Ubisoft's chief executive later described the Rayman team as "some people in Montpellier ... and some people in Paris" ([Game Developer, 2017](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/q-a-ceo-guillemot-on-ubisoft-s-origins-china-expansion)). Who did what, studio by studio, could not be settled.

The most instructive fact about its making is its twin. Ubi Soft built **Tonic Trouble** on the same engine, from a concept by the same man, with a larger team (about 120), and released it two months earlier, "to avoid damaging Rayman's reputation". On the N64 it averaged 53 per cent against Rayman 2's 89; its critics "criticized the camera system", and GamePro's main complaint was the controls "being incongruent with the camera" ([Wikipedia, Tonic Trouble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonic_Trouble)). The engine was reused again a year later for a Donald Duck game whose N64 version averaged about 70 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Duck:_Goin%27_Quackers)). Same tools, three results: what differed was the direction, and by every account the time spent on feel. Ancel's own verdicts, read in full: "I was really happy with Rayman 2", and "Time is more important than the number of people" ([Game Developer, 2014](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/-i-rayman-i-creator-sees-strength-in-small-development-teams)).

The N64 became the lead machine part-way through, "necessitating reducing the quality of the textures", and the game kept its reviews anyway: the look was built on shape and colour (the article names Miyazaki, Tex Avery and Art Nouveau as influences), which survives a low resolution ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayman_2:_The_Great_Escape)). The last clause is our reading.

## A small set of moves, tuned to forgive

Rayman runs, jumps, **slows his fall with his hair**, shoots, strafes while shooting, swings from floating rings, swims, carries and throws. Wikipedia gives the list: he "starts the game with minimal abilities, and he can gain more abilities as the game progresses"; he can "use his helicopter hair to slow his descent while jumping, with some segments later in the game allowing him to fly with his hair"; and "a strafing stance ... allows him to easily aim orbs whilst avoiding enemy attacks" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayman_2:_The_Great_Escape)). Second-hand, from the speedrunners' notes: he can catch ledges (and uses the hover to reach them), "can climb certain walls and ceilings", and holding a second button during the hover trades height for distance ("you will move forward a little faster. However, your descent will be steeper"). The same button that attacks also uses whatever is in front of him: he shoots a ring to swing from it, shoots a plum to move it. The N64 manual, seen only in summary, puts jump on A, the shot on B, the strafe on Z and the camera on the C buttons. A climb between two close walls, which the first researcher remembered, was confirmed by nothing.

What the makers wanted from this is the clearest thing in the research, though most of it is second-hand. Palmieri, as summarised from GamesRadar: **"We didn't want the challenge to come from the character controls or the camera management"**; the team "worked a lot on controls and on the camera to make it easy, fun and permissive enough"; and one small rule let Rayman walk a little past a platform's edge, which "let the player have more fun and fail less". Ancel, read in full and speaking in 2002, describes the method behind that:

> "You must look at the player's reflexes. To avoid frustration, you must think about what's natural for people. Test your new control with your friends, wife, children... A single delay on the buttons, the acceleration curve of the camera, all these parameters are important to tune if you want good control. You must have more than 100 of these kinds of parameters in your game, and must be able to change them easily depending on the player's feedback." ([Game Developer, 2002](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/game-design-secrets-of-the-sages----creating-characters-storyboarding-and-design-documents); the dash in the original is replaced by a comma here)

The studio later wrote the same priority down as doctrine: camera, character and controls come first, and "only once the main character was finished and comfortably fun to control did we begin working on level and gameplay element design" ([Game Developer, Origins](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/remaking-i-rayman-i-camera-character-controls)).

Two things qualify the picture. First, although Wikipedia says the game was "inspired by Super Mario 64", Ancel said in 2011 that Mario was no model for him: "I don't like gliding, I don't like its inertia, and I don't like not being able to give some slaps!" ([Game Developer, 2011](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-evolutionary-process-of-building-i-rayman-origins-i-)). He means Mario's sliding on after the stick is released. Rayman stops when told. Second, **the hover is what "permissive" means in practice**: a slow, steerable fall lets a player mend a misjudged jump after leaving the ground. That is our reading; no maker says it in those words. Critics agreed about the controls: Game Informer's writers, per Wikipedia, called it the best N64 game "because of its camera and control system".

## Twenty-odd linear levels, each with one other way of moving

"The player navigates through a mostly linear sequence of levels, fighting robotic pirate enemies, solving puzzles and collecting lums. Collecting enough lums gains the player access to new parts of the world" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayman_2:_The_Great_Escape)). Levels are reached from a hub map, the Hall of Doors; second-hand, the four big gates open at 100, 300, 475 and 550 of the game's 1,000 yellow lums, so the last gate asks for only 55 per cent. The story supplies a second, unmissable lock: four masks, three of them won in "sanctuary" levels, each of which has a guardian.

The variety was the aim. Besides Ancel's "unique game sequences", Game Informer picked out "helicoptering down a long chute or skiing behind a sea monster" (as Wikipedia reports it), and Palmieri says each level designer was handed "ingredients": his were "the walking rocket, or the updrafts allowing Rayman to ascend thanks to his helicopter ability, and the explosive barrel" (second-hand). Ancel's one-line summary of what he learned from Ubisoft's Serge Hascoët, said of game design in general, fits the structure exactly: "First you need to put the basis; then you may try to obtain the more different experiences you can get from this basis, while also working on rhythm. Basis, variety, rhythm" ([Game Developer, 2011](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-evolutionary-process-of-building-i-rayman-origins-i-)).

The rides themselves, as far as they could be traced. **Almost every cell is second-hand**, from fan wiki and speedrun pages seen only in summary; the few things read in full are marked "(read)". The notes file `rides_slides_and_level_structure.md` has the addresses.

## What the makers and their critics say a ride needs

Three statements bear on how to build one, and it matters which game each is about.

**Rare.** The studio's later rulebook calls a change of controls, camera or situation "exotic gameplay" and says: "for it to remain unique, it needs to be sparse in distribution throughout the game. Some exotic gameplay sequences only appear once over the course of an entire game" ([Game Developer, Origins](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/rational-design-the-core-of-i-rayman-origins-i-)). Rayman 2 fits, on the second-hand account above: the chair appears once, the ski once or twice, the flight once.

**Taught first.** The same article sets out three steps for anything new: show it where it cannot hurt, let the player practise it "in a safe area", then test it. The steps are "exposition, validation and challenge". Rayman 2's shell (enemy, hint, taming, ride) and its two chases (the Bayou, then the Precipice) look like this method long before it was written down. That match is our reading.

**The movement is already the pressure.** On a level where the screen drags the player forward, the Origins designer found that the forced motion "adds a lot to a player's anxiety level independent from the actual gameplay challenges", and so made the obstacles "almost as simple as the challenges found in the second or third level of the game ... the simplicity of the challenges balance it out and keep the level achievable but memorable." He adds a warning: a drastic change of play that is also "too challenging" throws off the whole game's curve. And on pursuit: "Many times in Rayman Origins did we force the player to move by pursuing him with a swarm of enemies, so as to change up the pacing" (same article).

Rayman 2's one unloved ride supports this from the other side. The flying shell in the last fight gives, in one reviewer's words (second-hand), "very little control", and a single hit restarts the phase; the Dreamcast version has "a reworked final battle" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayman_2:_The_Great_Escape)). The complaint is not that it is hard. It is that the player has little to do and loses a lot when it goes wrong.

A critic's view of pacing, read in full and not about Rayman, gives a way to judge any linear stretch: keep "the distance between important chunks of content relatively short", limit "the amount of busywork that needs to be repeated when a player dies", give "opportunities to rest ... which they can take advantage of at their discretion", vary "the length of periods they need to remain continuously engaged for", and let those periods "get longer in correspondence with the level of difficulty, ramping up to say a boss encounter". Her preferred timer is a thing that follows: it "rises at a steady rate, but if the player gets ahead of it, it will catch up" ([CritPoints, Pacing and Games](https://critpoints.net/2016/10/18/pacing-and-games/)).

## The camera: taken over where the path is fixed, and still the weak point

Rayman 2 "allows the player control over the camera, though in some situations this control is limited to only certain angles" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayman_2:_The_Great_Escape)). The makers' account is second-hand. Palmieri: "We also worked a lot on the 'level design cameras' to create game situations where the player didn't need to control the camera angle", which sometimes "even offered 2D gameplay situations in a 3D environment game". Ancel, in a magazine interview from the time, is summarised as saying the level artists avoided spaces so tight that the camera could not move (the summary's words, not his), and that the fall-back was a fixed camera looking down from high or up from low. Given that the team had begun the game as a 2D side-scroller, building side-on courses and then pointing a fixed 3D camera at them was probably habit as much as theory; that is our reading.

It did not satisfy everyone. One N64 review (second-hand) said the game "still suffers from one of the original problems with 3D games: the erratic camera"; the handheld ports were marked down for "camera problems" (read, Wikipedia); and when Miyamoto played Ancel's next 3D game he "wasn't satisfied with cameras" and "suggested we had a look at what Nintendo did with Super Mario Sunshine" ([Game Developer, 2011](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-evolutionary-process-of-building-i-rayman-origins-i-)). The fair summary: **the hand-set cameras on fixed paths are what was praised; the free camera in open places was ordinary for its day.**

One more point from the critic, about any level that runs away from the viewer: "aligning the camera with the primary plane the character is moving on helps players significantly with accurate movement across that plane", whereas a camera behind the hero "makes it difficult to judge how far things are ahead" ([CritPoints, on Crash Bandicoot](https://critpoints.net/2016/08/21/novacanoo-crash-bandicoot-reply/)). A view from behind is good for steering left and right and poor for judging distance.

## Fights are staged, not deep

Combat is one shot button, a held strafe, and later a charge. Tonic Trouble's team called Rayman 2 the "action" one of the pair, but the sequel's team thought otherwise: Rayman 3 was "redesigned around encounters with multiple enemies" with "faster-paced action" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayman_3:_Hoodlum_Havoc)). The bosses that could be traced are movement tests in costume: a slide with a guardian behind, a guardian under stalactites that can be shot down (second-hand), a ride. The studio's own later judgement on its bosses is the useful part: "A boss should also be there to challenge a player's understanding of the gameplay mechanics they have been taught during the previous world", and of Origins' bosses, "apart from the odd chase sequence ... the rest are quite lackluster" ([Game Developer, Origins](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/remaking-i-rayman-i-camera-character-controls)). RATQUEST's fighting, with its tells and openings, is already well past Rayman 2's; there is nothing to take here but that one sentence.

## A thousand lums, eighty cages

The collectibles are the part of Rayman 2 furthest from what the owner wants, and the report should be plain about that. There are 1,000 yellow lums (second-hand), strung through the levels, down the slides and along the keg flights. Their jobs: open the world at the low thresholds above; unlock "more information about the game world and its back story" (read); and, with every lum and cage in a level, earn a bonus race (read). Cages matter more: "Part of the lums are hidden in small cages, in which other freedom fighters or Teensies are imprisoned" (read), there are eighty, and every tenth lengthens the life bar (second-hand). The other lum colours are not prizes but functions wearing one shape: red heals, green is a checkpoint, blue is air, purple is a ring to swing from, silver is a new power (second-hand).

No critic was found saying anything about the lum-collecting, for or against. In the studio's later games the common pickup is openly a signpost: "The main path shown by lums" leads one way and secrets sit off it ([Game Developer, on Legends](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/secrets-design-in-rayman-legends)), and "a player can finish a level without a single Lum" ([Game Developer, Origins](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/rational-design-the-core-of-i-rayman-origins-i-)). The same Legends analysis notes two aids worth remembering: prisoners are **heard before they are seen** ("we hear cries for help"), and the count is shown as places in order, so "you instantly know ... approximately where, you've missed something."

## A handful of friends, each one a job with a face

The cast is small and every member does something the game needs. From Wikipedia's character list: Globox is "Rayman's dimwitted best friend", easily frightened, who delivers powers; Murfy is "a green bottle fly who serves as a guide", with "a snarky, sarcastic nature", who "appears to be bored with his job", and whose instructions are optional; the Teensies run the hub and, once freed, "they're having trouble remembering who is the king"; Ly hands out powers and vanishes; a snake is a vehicle; a whale is a guide; a weakened warrior is a gate ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayman)). The crowd is implied, not shown: eighty cages, and a friend with "over 650 children".

Ancel's own account of how a character reaches the player, read in full, gives an order:

> "The visual aspect is important for the first impact. After this first feeling, the next one is about animation. A lot of the personality is revealed by the animations, the way your character move in common actions. The next and most important step, especially for games, comes from his powers, his specific actions... The next and deepest aspect will come from his feelings, his personality, the way he reacts in particular situations: danger, love, surprises, victory." ([Game Developer, 2002](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/game-design-secrets-of-the-sages----creating-characters-storyboarding-and-design-documents); dashes replaced by commas)

He wanted "an easy-to-animate character" (Rayman has no arms or legs to animate), sums the series up as "50 percent action and 50 percent humor", and describes one friend by a single contradiction: "a powerful but fearful friend". Second-hand: in the N64, PC and Dreamcast versions every creature speaks invented gibberish with subtitles, each kind with its own sound, and a reviewer found the wit "created almost exclusively through the character animations". No maker was found explaining the gibberish; that it was cheap is our guess.

## What this means for RATQUEST

Each suggestion names what in the game it touches and what it rests on. "Read" means a page read in full; "Origins" means the studio's later 2D game, read in full; "second-hand" means a search summary of a page nobody here opened; "opinion" means the critic; "our reading" means a conclusion drawn here. All of it is within the standing rules in START-HERE.md: nothing below makes the game easier, turns the view by itself, puts up a banner or gives the hero a voice. Nothing in the game has been changed.

### Where the game already stands

The Long Drop is not a Rayman 2 ride. It is 1,386 units and ninety-one seconds on the shield, where Rayman 2's nearest things are a chute in the middle of a level and a chair that is half of a short one. The owner asked for exactly that ("a whole level where you're on the shield board sliding the whole time"), so the first researcher's idea of breaking it up with stretches on foot should be dropped. Two things are already right by Rayman 2's lights: nothing stands across the rider's way but a weed, and a bail costs a moment and no hearts, so the ride is hard through speed and not through fiddly obstacles or heavy penalties. And one thing matches by accident: Rayman 2's best-described slide ends on a ramp that throws him into the air and into a boss, which is the Long Drop's ending.

### The downhill ride and other set pieces

**1. Put each of the Long Drop's pieces somewhere smaller first.** Rayman 2's shell is an enemy before it is a ride; its stream slide comes long before its hard slides; its mild chase comes long before its full one. Today the only places the knight meets a slope, a rail, a kicker or a pipe are the Move Garden and the Long Drop, and both are test rooms outside the game. The cave that is still on the list (step three of the slopes job) is the natural home: a slope too steep to stand on with a soft landing, where he first slides on his hide; a short run where ATTACK in the air first puts the shield under him; one rail over nothing dangerous; one kicker. Each alone, each once, nothing combined. Then the Long Drop is the test. *Rests on*: the shell's and the chases' lead-up (second-hand); "exposition, validation and challenge" (Origins); the match between them (our reading).

**2. Arrange `COURSE` as acts, one new thing an act, each with its own look.** The course is already a list of sections (pipe, flat, bed, ledge). Order them so that each kind of obstacle first appears alone and wide (one weed in an empty bed, one low rail beside the lane, one kicker with nothing after it), then narrower, then mixed with the last, and so that the stretches between flats grow longer toward the giant ramp. The flats are the only breathing places the course has, and at riding speed a flat of 14 is gone in about a second: an act that should end on a breath needs a longer one, or a gentle bed with nothing in it. Give each act a different place (the owner has already named them: the disused pipe with slime in its bottom, the broken cave, the underground railway), since "one sewer theme for the whole length" is on the level's own not-done list and Rayman 2's chair ride is said to pass from treetops to forest to pipes in one go. *Rests on*: "Basis, variety, rhythm" (read, said of design in general); periods of engagement that lengthen toward a boss (opinion); the chair ride's changing scenery (second-hand).

**3. If it needs to be harder, make it faster and tighter, not stranger.** The Origins rule is that forced motion is itself the pressure, so the obstacle shapes stay simple. That is not a case for softening anything: it says where the difficulty should live. Tighten lanes, shorten the run-in after a ledge, lengthen the unbroken stretches. Do not add new kinds of hazard late in the ride, and do not add anything that ends the ride in one touch. *Rests on*: the forced-scroll passage (Origins); the flying shell complaint (second-hand, one reviewer).

**4. Put something behind him for one act only.** The chase is the one set piece Ancel himself named, and the studio later used pursuit "to change up the pacing". For the last act before the giant ramp: something comes down the tunnel after him (a surge of the slime, which is waiting for a job; or a train, in the railway act) that keeps a steady pace, so that it only matters to a rider who bails or brakes. Because the view may not turn to show it, it has to be told from behind: its noise, its light thrown forward along the walls, its shadow, rumble in the pad. Use it once. This makes that act harder, not easier, and it is an addition the owner has not asked for, so it is his to pick. *Rests on*: Ancel's "chased by a pirate spaceship" (read); pursuit as pacing (Origins); a pursuer that catches up (opinion). How Rayman 2 shows its own pursuers was not found.

**5. When the sword comes back to the board, make its targets part of the lane.** The owner's back-burner idea is one-hit enemies to swing at while riding; at present a rider cannot swing at all. Rayman 2's rides keep the ordinary verbs, and on its chased slide the things he shoots are obstacles in his path (crystals, thrown skulls), not a side game. So: a thing in the lane that is a wall unless cut in time, placed where weeds are placed now. One button meaning what it always means. *Rests on*: the Cave of Bad Dreams slide (second-hand); the shell's jump on the jump button (second-hand).

**6. Let the foot of the ride remember that he got there, and let the Widow ask for something the ride taught.** START-HERE says a level that is not a fall-back level puts him back at its start. If that holds for the Long Drop (it was not tested here), a death to the Widow costs ninety-one seconds of ride before each new attempt. Rayman 2's most resented moment is exactly a ride tied to a boss with a harsh restart. A restart point at the arena would leave the fight as hard as it is and take away only the repetition; that is the owner's call. Separately, the studio's own rule is that a boss should test what the level before it taught. The Widow is fought on foot on a flat floor, so the cheapest link is the entrance: let how he arrives matter (a landed kickflip off the giant ramp, or a dive out of the fall, as her first opening). Whether a rider in the air can dive was not checked. *Rests on*: one hit restarts the phase (second-hand); "limiting the amount of busywork that needs to be repeated when a player dies" (opinion); the boss rule (Origins).

**7. Keep the "other way of moving" rare everywhere else.** Rayman 2 has many set pieces but each appears once or twice. The shield ride is RATQUEST's one exotic thing, and it will stay special if slopes long enough to ride are few: the cave, the Long Drop, perhaps one run in a later world. A rideable slope in every hall would spend it. *Rests on*: "sparse in distribution" (Origins).

### Pacing a linear level

Most of RATQUEST is open halls of wedges, after Mario 64, and Rayman 2 has little to say to those. It speaks to the linear places: the cave to come, the Long Drop, and side areas such as the Lift Shaft, the Leap of Faith and the Lightless Way. For those, Rayman 2's pattern is **walk, one new situation, a breath, the same situation harder, out**, with at most one stretch of forced motion. The test to apply to each: is there one thing here the player does nowhere else, was it shown safely first, and is there a place to stand still before the hardest part? *Rests on*: the level structure (read, Wikipedia, in outline; second-hand in detail); the pacing list (opinion).

### The camera on a ride

Rayman 2's answer to a ride is to take the camera from the player and aim it by hand. Half of that is closed to this game: the view turns only on the right stick, and the left stick steers relative to the view, so a ride camera that swung to a side-on angle would change what the stick means in mid-carve. What remains open is everything except the turn.

- **Give each `COURSE` section a view of its own**: distance, height and tilt, set by hand, as it already has a `lane`. In a pipe, low and close, for speed. On a bed with kickers and gaps, higher and further back and tipped down, because from behind "it's difficult to judge how far things are ahead", and height is the only way to show distance without turning.
- **Lead him over every lip.** Before a ledge and on the giant ramp, bring the view forward and up and tip it down so the landing is in the picture as he leaves the ground. This is the same fault START-HERE records at the garden's chute (he drops out of the bottom of the picture), and its cure is the same: how fast the view comes forward and down. Put that speed on a slider.
- **Keep every ride straight, as the Long Drop is.** Bends are on the not-done list because of the camera rule. Leave them there. A lane can wander from side to side across a wide bed without the heading ever changing. A real change of direction belongs where he is off the board (the fall into the arena is one).
- **Do not design anything on a ride that needs a side view.** Rayman 2's "2D situations in 3D" came from fixed side cameras. Seen from behind at a fixed heading, the Long Drop is a different kind of flat course: left and right, and when to jump. Ask only those two things of the rider. The crumbs hung along each kicker's real arc, and shadows on the ground, already do the work a side view would.

*Rests on*: camera "limited to only certain angles" (read, Wikipedia); Palmieri and Ancel on designed cameras (second-hand); the camera-plane remark (opinion); the mixed reviews (second-hand, and Miyamoto's remark, read).

### Movement: no glide for the knight

Rayman's hover is the heart of his forgiving feel, and it is the wrong thing to copy. The knight already reaches 4.6 up with a double jump and 5.7 with a catch; the owner's complaint is that he "can just jump over stuff"; he has ridden the Move Garden and decided the numbers stay, praising the wall kick, double jump and ledge catch "with nothing to spare" because "it required skill". A slowed fall would add distance to every jump, take the commitment out of the catch, and mean re-measuring every gap `reach.ts` knows. The critic makes the same case from the other side, praising a recent 3D platformer where "you have no mechanical way to stall your fall" and the game makes you "simply jump correctly" ([CritPoints](https://critpoints.net/2024/06/27/the-big-catch-tacklebox-review/)). The first researcher's "brief shield-glide" should be dropped for the same reason.

Three smaller things from Rayman 2's movement are worth keeping:

- **If a float is ever wanted, make it a place and not a button.** Rayman 2 does this itself: updrafts are a level ingredient that lift him, and free flight is lent for one level and taken back. A vent that holds up a rider over one gap in the Long Drop would be a set piece; a glide button would be a new jump set.
- **The 0.1 second of grace after running off an edge is this game's version of Palmieri's "walk a little past the edge".** None was found in Mario 64's code. It is there already; leave it as it is.
- **Ancel's hundred parameters are the Tweaks panel.** His method is to keep every delay and curve adjustable and tune them on other people's hands. The ride, the grind, the slide and the Long Drop have been ridden by tests and not by a person with a controller. Before more is built on them, that is the missing step, and the camera's come-forward speed belongs among the sliders.

*Rests on*: the hover (read, Wikipedia); updrafts and lent flight (second-hand); the edge rule (second-hand); the parameters quote (read); the owner's decision (START-HERE).

### Collectibles

Rayman 2 would have the owner picking up a thousand of something, so take its uses and not its counts.

- **On a ride, a line of crumbs is a sign, and should be only as long as the sign needs.** The Long Drop has 235 crumbs: 108 along the ground line, 74 in the air off kickers, 53 over rails. The air and rail ones are earned by a jump or a grind, which is the owner's rule kept. The 108 on the ground are Rayman's lums-as-signpost. Keep short runs of them where the lane is not obvious (through a slalom, into a kicker) and trade the rest for a few hunks worth the same in all (the `worth` prop the Cistern uses), each on the harder line: above a kicker where only a popped jump reaches, at the end of the longest rail, on the far side of a rail-to-rail hop.
- **If the Long Drop becomes a level with wedges, Rayman 2 suggests their kinds**: reach the arena; beat the Widow; all the hunks in one ride (a small fixed number, like its remake's twenty red lums on the chair); a ride with no bail; a ride against the clock (its "Walk of Life", entered from the hub, is exactly a timed run of a course already learned). None of these is a sweep.
- **Let a hidden prize be heard before it is seen.** The Mario 64 report noted that the view sees only five to seven units up a wall, so a prize at the top of a chimney is invisible from its foot. The later Rayman games give every hidden prisoner a voice that carries. A sound that grows as he nears a prize fits the no-banner rule and costs one loop.
- **Do not copy the life bar that grows with rescues.** It makes the game easier and nobody has asked for it.
- **The door shares need nothing.** Rayman 2's last gate at 55 per cent is close to the Keep door's 25 of 47.

*Rests on*: lums and cages (read in outline, second-hand in number); lums as path markers and voices for prisoners (read, a player's analysis of a later game); the red-lum chair run and the timed walks (second-hand); the owner's words on collectibles (START-HERE).

### A small cast of odd creatures

The Club now has a dozen and more characters, some of whom sit, drink, play cards and tend bar, and it grows as worlds are cleared. Rayman 2's cast suggests what to add next, and it is not more creatures.

- **Give each regular one job the game needs and one contradiction.** Globox is "powerful but fearful"; Murfy is the guide who is bored of guiding; the Teensie kings cannot remember which of them is king. One line each, and the animation does the rest. Sir Hobnail the doorman, Madam Tawny behind the bar and the foxes at cards have jobs already; what each lacks is the one trait that is funny against the job (a huge doorman who flinches, a card player who peeks when the other looks away). Show it in the idle loop, per Ancel's order: looks, then how he moves, then what he does, and only last what he says.
- **Make one of them the Murfy for the shield.** The ride, the grind and the kickflip have no teacher in the game. Rayman 2's teacher is optional, short, rude, and found by walking up to him. A regular who has plainly ridden a shield himself, who explains it only if spoken to and with an attitude, does this without a banner and suits a silent hero, who can be talked at.
- **Let a regular be met in a level before he is at the bar.** The snake is freed and then tows him; the cages hold people. The Club already has "it grows" (`when`), but each newcomer simply appears. Meeting one first in the world (the creature who rides the Long Drop ahead of him and is at the bar afterwards, say) costs one placement and makes the arrival a small story with no cutscene. The room must still be full from the first minute, as the owner has ruled; this is for the extras.
- **One sound per kind of creature.** The regulars speak in "a borrowed recording at his own pitch", and voices of their own are on the not-done list. Rayman 2's answer for a whole cast was invented speech with a different texture for each kind and subtitles for the sense. Decide the texture by build (turtle, fox, ferret, owl, ant, stag) and the pitch by individual, and the talk box does the rest.
- **Keep scenes short and skippable.** Rayman 2 stops play for its dialogue scenes; the same team later worked to the rule "If you have anything to tell, you'd better have the player living it". For the little cutscene tool on the list, that argues for scenes that happen in the room as he passes, and for any scene that takes the controls being one he can leave.

*Rests on*: the character list (read, Wikipedia); Ancel on character and on the fearful friend (read); the snake and the cages (second-hand, cages read); gibberish by kind (second-hand, three agreeing summaries); the cinematics rule (read; said about a 2005 game).

### What to leave behind

The hover. The lum count and the lum gates. The camera that takes itself to a chosen angle. The automatic jump at a slide's edge (the Long Drop's kickers already throw a rider, and a jump on the lip is where its skill is). The one-hit restart. On-foot breaks in a level meant to be ridden end to end.

## Conclusion

Rayman 2 is remembered as a game of rides, but what the evidence shows is a game of **ordinary levels with one ride in each**, taught beforehand, played on the ordinary buttons, filmed by the designer, and over quickly. The Long Drop is a bigger bet than any of them, and the camera rule means it cannot lean on Rayman 2's favourite crutch. That is no reason to shrink it. It is a reason to give it what Rayman 2's rides get from their surroundings and the Long Drop has to supply for itself: an earlier, smaller meeting with every piece; acts that each add one thing; a view set by hand for each act without ever turning; one stretch with something behind him; and an ending that does not make him pay for the boss with the ride. On movement the answer is short: Rayman's glide suits Rayman's levels, and the knight's levels are being built on the opposite idea. On the cast, the lesson is one that costs almost nothing: one job and one joke apiece, shown in how they move.

How much of this is solid: the plain facts about the game, Ancel's own words from 2002, 2011 and 2014, and the studio's later method were read in full; the level-by-level detail, the lum numbers, the controls and the level designer's account were not. The single most useful thing still unread is the GamesRadar retrospective with Olivier Palmieri, then the game's manual and press kit. If the network ever allows them, they would firm up the ride table and the camera section. They are unlikely to change the list above, most of which rests on what the owner has already decided and on what the game already is.
