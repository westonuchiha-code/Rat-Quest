## Question 5. World and characters

### Takeaway
The world is small in cast and large in implied life: a captured population (prisoners in cages, hundreds of Globox's children, the Teensie kings), a handful of named friends who each hand Rayman something he needs, a sleeping god, and a comic robot villain. It was made to feel alive cheaply: every creature speaks an invented gibberish with subtitles instead of recorded dialogue (in the N64, PC and Dreamcast versions), personality is carried by animation, back story is unlocked by the main collectible, and the tutorial is a character with an attitude rather than a text box.

### Cited Findings

Who lives in it

- READ. Setting: "The Glade of Dreams is invaded by robotic pirates, led by Admiral Razorbeard, with the intent to conquer and enslave." The pirates have broken the world's core and captured most of its fighters. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayman_2:_The_Great_Escape
- READ. Globox: "a toad-like creature who is Rayman's dimwitted best friend and sidekick"; easily frightened but brave when it counts; "He and his wife Uglette have over 650 children." - READ. The Teensies: "a magical race of ancient, diminutive and wise creatures created by Polokus". Running joke in this game: after Rayman frees four of them, "they're having trouble remembering who is the king." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayman
- READ. How the guide character teaches

- READ. Murfy is "a 'flying encyclopaedia' who provides explanations on all kinds of gameplay elements", and his instructions are "(optional)". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayman_2:_The_Great_Escape
- SEARCH SUMMARY. How he is called: a fan wiki says he introduces mechanics and items and "the player can summon him by standing on his corresponding tile". Speedrun notes show his text can be skipped by pressing A repeatedly. https://breezewiki.discard.no/hero/wiki/Murfy and https://kb.speeddemosarchive.com/Rayman_2:_The_Great_Escape/The_Woods_of_Light
- SEARCH SUMMARY. The tutorial is a comedy sketch: Murfy reads the rules from a manual; the Manual itself "grows angry at Murfy's rudeness" and starts speaking through subtitles, threatening to fire him if he does not follow "the script". https://raymanpc.com/wiki/en/Manual
- SEARCH SUMMARY. He also sets up mechanics inside the fiction: in the Menhir Hills he warns about the walking shell and hints it can be tamed. https://raymanpc.com/wiki/en/Menhir_Hills
- SEARCH SUMMARY. The first level is safe: the Woods of Light is "a tutorial with no enemies". https://raymanpc.com/wiki/en/Clearing
- SEARCH SUMMARY. On PS1 Murfy "no longer acts as the player's tutorial"; Ly does it. https://raymanpc.com/wiki/en/Rayman_2_(PlayStation)
- READ (about Rayman Origins, a different game). The studio's written teaching method: tease a mechanic safely, let the player practise, then test: "exposition, validation and challenge". https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/rational-design-the-core-of-i-rayman-origins-i-

### Inferences (mine)
- Gibberish plus subtitles is the cheapest possible "full voice": no translation recording for five languages, no lip sync, a handful of sounds per species, and yet every character has a voice and a mood. It also fits cartridge space on the N64. That it is cheap is my reasoning; no maker said so in what I could reach.
- Every friend is a function. Globox delivers a power, Ly delivers powers and runs the time trials, the Teensies run the hub, Ssssam is a vehicle, Clark opens a gate, Murfy is the manual. The cast is small because each one is also a game system with a face.
- The population is mostly off-screen or caged. "650 children" and eighty cages imply a crowded world while the game only ever animates a few characters at once, which matches the frame-rate limit the Dreamcast team described.
- Making the tutorial character bored and rude lets the game be brief: a sarcastic one-liner is shorter than a polite explanation, and being optional means experienced players lose nothing.

### Gaps
- No maker statement on the reason for gibberish.
- I could not confirm from a read page which exact versions carry which voice option; this rests on three agreeing search summaries.
- "The General" is unconfirmed (see above).
- Nothing found on how many unique character models or animations the game has.

## Question 6. Development

### Takeaway
Rayman 2 began in early 1996 as a 2D sequel made by six people, was restarted as a 3D game after the industry's turn to 3D, and shipped in October 1999 after more than two years of 3D work by 70 to 80 people, many of them new to the job. It shared technology with Ubi Soft's other 3D platformer, Tonic Trouble, which was released first and reviewed far worse. The N64 became the lead platform part-way through, which forced lower-quality textures; the Dreamcast version restored them. It was a critical high point (around 90 percent on every original platform) whose exact sales I could not establish. Michel Ancel directed; the claim that a "Toshiaki Kato" directed it is a mix-up with a different Retro Gamer article.

### Cited Findings

The 2D prototype and the move to 3D

- READ. "Development on the prototype began in early 1996 with a team of six people and a budget of 10 million francs." It was planned for PlayStation, Saturn and Windows in late 1996. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayman_2:_The_Great_Escape
- READ. The 2D version (previewed July 1996) had "new mechanics allowing Rayman to move between foreground and background sections", robot invaders polluting the world, the fairy Betilla and a scientist named Fudge. It used "prerendered bitmaps of 3D computer models". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayman_2:_The_Great_Escape
- READ. The switch: "With the rise of 3D games, the team changed direction and turned it into a 3D platformer inspired by Super Mario 64." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayman_2:_The_Great_Escape
- READ. One level of the 2D prototype is playable in the PS1 version: Wikipedia's Rayman 2 article lists "a playable prototype of an early 2D version" among PS1 features; the series article adds it unlocks "if the player completes a certain percentage of the game" (tagged citation needed). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayman_2:_The_Great_Escape and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayman
- SEARCH SUMMARY. The RayWiki gives the unlock as 720 of the PS1 version's 800 Yellow Lums (90 percent) plus beating the final boss. https://raymanpc.com/wiki/en/Rayman_2_(PlayStation)

Engine and Tonic Trouble

- READ. "To avoid damaging Rayman's reputation, Ubisoft first released Tonic Trouble, a 3D platformer whose game engine would also be used in Rayman 2." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayman_2:_The_Great_Escape
- READ. Tonic Trouble: "conceptualized by Michel Ancel and developed by a team of around 120 people" at Ubi Soft Montreal from June 1996; its engine, "Architecture Commune Programmation", was "built by fifty in-house staff over a course of eighteen months" at a cost of four million US dollars. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonic_Trouble
- READ. The two were deliberately different: Tonic Trouble focused "more strongly on adventure, whereas Rayman 2 relied more on action". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonic_Trouble
- READ. Same technology, very different results: Tonic Trouble (N64, 31 August 1999) scored 53 percent on GameRankings; IGN's reviewer called it the "S. S. Minnow" to Rayman 2's "Love Boat"; even at the October 1998 Tokyo Game Show a critic noted Rayman 2 "looked 'significantly sharper'". It still "sold 1.1 million copies as of 2001". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonic_Trouble
- SEARCH SUMMARY (GamesRadar). Palmieri recalls the two teams were separate but exchanged ideas; lead artist Florent Sacré says the research done for Tonic Trouble shaped Rayman 2's technology. Same GamesRadar URL as above.

### Inferences (mine)
- Tonic Trouble is the control experiment for Rayman 2. Same publisher, same year, shared technology, a larger team (about 120 against 70 to 80), a concept from the same man, and a score roughly forty points lower, with critics naming the camera and controls. The difference was not the engine. It is reasonable to credit the direction: the camera work, the "permissive" tuning and the variety of situations the makers talk about.
- Starting in 2D left marks. A team that had designed side-on obstacle courses kept building them, then pointed a fixed 3D camera at them. "2D gameplay situations in a 3D environment" is probably as much the team's history as a theory.
- The art direction did the work the hardware could not. Lowering texture quality for the N64 did not sink the game because the style (soft, painterly, mythic) survives low resolution. A realistic look would not have.
- Ancel's "Basis, variety, rhythm" is close to a one-line summary of Rayman 2's structure: a small move set (basis), a new situation every level (variety), and rides and chases to change speed (rhythm).

### Gaps
- Sales of Rayman 2 itself: not established.
- The exact split of work between Montpellier, Paris, Annecy and other studios: not established.
- Retro Gamer issue 275 and the GamesRadar article were not read; neither was the press kit or the Devs Play video.
- No budget figure for the 3D game (only the 10 million francs for the 1996 2D prototype).
- No maker statement found on why the first game's cast and Betilla were dropped.

## Ideas this suggests for RatQuest

Suggestions of mine, tied to the findings above.

- Give the Club's regulars invented speech with subtitles. Rayman 2's N64, PC and Dreamcast versions voice every creature in gibberish, with a different sound per species. For a PS1-style game this is authentic to the period, cheap, needs no translation recording, and lets every character at the bar "talk". Give each regular one vocal texture (squeak, rumble, whistle).
- Make every named character also a system. Globox delivers powers, the Teensies run the hub, the snake is a vehicle, the fly is the manual. In the Club: the barkeep opens levels, one regular teaches moves, one sells or stores things, one is the ride (whoever lends or tunes the shield).
- Let the tutorial character have an attitude and be optional. Murfy is sarcastic, brief, skippable, and summoned by standing on a marked spot. A bored bar regular who explains a move only if you sit at his table would do the same job.
- Imply a crowd, animate a few. "650 children" and eighty caged prisoners make a busy world on a console that can show only a few characters at once. RatQuest can fill the Club as prisoners are freed: empty stools first, more regulars later.
- Pay out lore with the main collectible, as Rayman 2 does, rather than in mandatory scenes.
- Pick a look that survives low resolution. Rayman 2 lost texture quality to the N64 and kept its reviews. RatQuest is already PS1-style: lean on shape, colour and silhouette for the sewer and railway, not texture detail.
- Take the Tonic Trouble lesson: shared tools and a bigger team did not make the better game. Spend time on the camera and on the feel of the rat rather than on more content. Ancel's own line, read on Game Developer: "Time is more important than the number of people."
- Use "Basis, variety, rhythm" as the test for the downhill level: the basis is the rat's existing moves, the variety is one new situation (the long ride), the rhythm is where it falls between slower levels reached from the Club.
- Keep the 2D instinct. A team that came from 2D built side-on courses and framed them in 3D. For rails and half-pipes, a side-on or fixed framing is not a compromise, it is the thing that made this game readable.

### New findings

Ancel, near the time, on how a character is made (a maker's statement, read in full)
- READ. On Rayman's design: "When I created Rayman, I didn't really analyze it. I just made it like this because it was fun for me and my friends. I also wanted an easy-to-animate character. Your character must not look like [other characters], but at the same time he must appear familiar to people." Marc Saltzman, "Game Design: Secrets of the Sages", excerpted on Game Developer, 15 March 2002. https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/game-design-secrets-of-the-sages----creating-characters-storyboarding-and-design-documents
- READ (same passage). The order in which a player meets a character: "The visual aspect is important for the first impact. After this first feeling, the next one is about animation. A lot of the personality is revealed by the animations—the way your character move in common actions. The next and most important step—especially for games—comes from his powers, his specific actions ... The next and deepest aspect will come from his feelings, his personality, the way he reacts in particular situations: danger, love, surprises, victory".
- READ (same passage). "Rayman is 50 percent action and 50 percent humor."
- READ (same passage). A friend defined by one contradiction: among the sequences the team set out to invent was "cooperating with a powerful but fearful friend". (I take this to be Globox, whom Wikipedia describes as easily frightened but brave when it counts; the identification is mine.)
- READ (the 2011 interview, re-read). What Ancel later learned from Serge Hascoët about story, during King Kong (about 2004, so AFTER Rayman 2, which does use cutscenes): "We're done putting cinematics in video games ... If you have anything to tell, you'd better have the player living it." https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-evolutionary-process-of-building-i-rayman-origins-i-

"The General": confirmed second-hand
- SEARCH SUMMARY (RayWiki). "At a very late point in Rayman 2, Razorbeard is visited by the General, a character who first appeared in Tonic Trouble", who sells him the Grolgoth in one short cutscene; his sales patter is summarised as the machine doing "everything but the dishes". So the first pass's recollection was right, and the character is a one-scene salesman borrowed from the sister game. https://raymanpc.com/wiki/en/Grolgoth

Where the team sat
- READ. Ubisoft's chief executive, 2017, on the first big internal team: "That's how we created a big team for Rayman. We then had some people in Montpellier—Michel Ancel was in Montpellier—and some people in Paris." (Mathew Kumar's interview with Yves Guillemot, 9 August 2017.) This supports the first pass's reading that the 70 to 80 people were split between Montpellier and the Paris area; it is said of "Rayman" in general, not of the second game by name. https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/q-a-ceo-guillemot-on-ubisoft-s-origins-china-expansion
- READ. Frédéric Houde, the studio's co-founder, turns up by name in the 2004 Game Developers Choice nominations as a designer of Beyond Good & Evil ("Michel Ancel, Jacques Exertier, Frédéric Houde, and Sébastien Morin for game design"). Nothing read says what he did on Rayman 2. https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/igda-announces-nominees-more-award-winners

The engine after Rayman 2
- READ. It was reused for a licensed game a year later: Ubi Soft Casablanca built Donald Duck: Goin' Quackers for Windows, Dreamcast and N64 "on an optimized Rayman 2 engine", with music by Daniel Masson (the N64 Rayman 2's composer). IGN's reviewer, as Wikipedia reports him, thought it looked worse than Rayman 2 on the same engine. Another small proof that the engine was not what made Rayman 2 good. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Duck:_Goin%27_Quackers
- READ. Rayman 2 and Tonic Trouble were added to Nintendo's N64 Classics library on the same day, 17 December 2025. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nintendo_Classics (Nintendo's own store page for that library names both games only in its trademark lines: https://www.nintendo.com/us/store/products/nintendo-64-nintendo-classics-switch/ )

The studio's later games, on teaching and on secrets (different games; players' analyses on Game Developer's blogs, read in full)
- READ (Jorge Albor, 9 February 2012, on Rayman Origins). Powers are handed over one world at a time, as in Rayman 2: "Unlock an ability, then work your way through a series of levels using that ability", and "Rayman Origins rarely leaves previous abilities behind." He quotes its director Sébastien Morin (from an NZGamer interview I could not read): "we populated the game with a lot of (really, I mean really) hard challenges, that can be done, when you're ready for it." https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/gated-learning-in-rayman-origins-
- READ (Stanislav Costiuc, 20 April 2016, on Rayman Legends). Prisoners are found by ear: "Whenever we're not far from a captured teensie or a secret room with one, we hear cries for help." And the count is drawn as places, not a number: "you instantly know when, and most importantly approximately where, you've missed something." https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/secrets-design-in-rayman-legends
