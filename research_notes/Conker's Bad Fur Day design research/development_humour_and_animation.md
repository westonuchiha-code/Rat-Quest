## Question 6. Development: from Twelve Tales to Bad Fur Day, the team, the timeline, cut content, sales, the remake

### Takeaway
The game began in 1996 as a cute Mario 64-style platformer, was shown in 1997 and 1998, was mocked for being another cute platformer next to Rare's own Banjo-Kazooie, and stalled amid team quarrels. An artist on it, Chris Seavor, pitched "Bad Fur Day", was made project lead, and rebuilt it around one rule found by accident: each mission is a set-up, a bit of play, and an outrageous punchline. It shipped in March 2001, about four years after it started, reviewed very well and sold badly. The 2005 Xbox remake looked better, played a little easier and was more censored.

### Inferences (mine)
- The turnaround did not come from a new plan. It came from one fix to one mission (guns on a beehive) that made the bosses laugh, followed by "make more of that". The team then had a test for every scene: does it end on a punchline.
- What rescued the project was a clear point of difference, not better platforming. The engine, the hero and much of the world were kept.
- The method (no script, verbal design, notepads) worked because the team was small, sat together, and had fast tools. The same method is what the cancelled version had been failing with; the difference was one person with the final say on tone.
- Critical success and poor sales had separate causes: the game was good, and it was an expensive adult game on a dying family console that its own publisher would not promote.

### Gaps
- Team size: no number found anywhere I could read. Only names and the remark that the remake's team was bigger.
- The date Seavor took over is not stated; sources disagree on whether he joined before or after E3 1998.
- Lifetime sales: no reliable figure.
- I could not read the Retro Gamer making-of, the Eurogamer profile, the Kotaku pieces, the Nintendo Life piece, or hear the commentary. Any nuance in those is missing here.
- "Built chapter by chapter": I found no source saying this in those words. What Wikipedia supports is that each mission scenario was made voice first, to a formula, with the overall story written out of order.

## Question 6, continued. How the humour is carried by animation, voice and timing

### Takeaway
The comedy was recorded before it was built: Seavor and Beanland improvised voices in the studio, and scenes were animated and cut to that audio. Timing was controlled by putting jokes where the player cannot change the pace (cutscenes, speech bubbles cut to camera angles) and by sound choices made for laughs (a frying pan because it clangs). Animators were given time for small reactive details. The price was about two hours of cutscenes and a slow editing loop.

### Cited Findings
- **READ** Voice first. Quote: "For making each mission scenario, voice recording came first". One session lasted an hour. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conker%27s_Bad_Fur_Day
- **READ** Almost nothing was scripted. Quote: "Out of all the game's dialogue, only the intro was scripted." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conker%27s_Bad_Fur_Day
- **READ** The punchline is a staged scene. Quote: "an "extreme punchline" cutscene as reward for completing the task" (inner quotation marks are Wikipedia's). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conker%27s_Bad_Fur_Day
- **READ** Timing was adjusted by hand against the camera. Quote: "adjustments to the timing of speech bubbles to match camera angle changes". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conker%27s_Bad_Fur_Day
- **READ** A sound effect chose the weapon. Quote: "because it justified the use of a comic sound effect of a metal object hitting something". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conker%27s_Bad_Fur_Day
- **READ** Detail animation as a priority, even when tedious. Quote: "having to animate each of several bricks on a bridge separately". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conker%27s_Bad_Fur_Day
- **READ** Animators learned the real thing. Quote: "Animating Conker's juggling required Ridgeway being taught by David Rose how to juggle." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conker%27s_Bad_Fur_Day
- **READ** Output was slow by any standard: "8.7 seconds" of animation a day, by the animators' estimate. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conker%27s_Bad_Fur_Day
- **READ** Music was written to react. For the Matrix parody, "shooting bullets would fade the channels of the upbeat music into channels playing something more downtempo". A level theme is arranged from fart noises, after a South Park song. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conker%27s_Bad_Fur_Day
- **READ** The showpiece song was written around a team member's skill. The Great Mighty Poo's aria was written for Chris Marlow, "who had experience in opera", and "recorded in a single afternoon". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conker%27s_Bad_Fur_Day
- **READ** The stated models were Monty Python (a running gag chosen for being "trivial and banal") and South Park. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conker%27s_Bad_Fur_Day
- **READ** Rating limits shaped the staging: for the urination attack, "only the back of him was animated". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conker%27s_Bad_Fur_Day
- **READ** Critics credited delivery as much as writing. Quote: "Most reviewers agreed the jokes were clever, funny, and well-delivered". Others found it "juvenile, misogynistic, and over-filled with profanity and bodily humor". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conker%27s_Bad_Fur_Day
- **READ** A composer's essay on Game Developer, quoting critic Christopher Gates with Conker among his examples: funny games lean on audio because timing needs control. Quote: "easier to control the comedic timing when the player can't control the pace". https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/comedic-sound-for-the-game-music-composer
- **READ** A 2004 Game Developer feature on comedy games describes the usual method, which is Conker's too. Quote: "rely on familiar gameplay mechanics with a healthy sprinkling of mini-cinemas and voice calls to provide comic relief". https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/funny-me-on-leisure-suit-larry-magna-cum-laude-and-the-search-for-humor-in-gaming
- **READ** An academic-style Game Developer essay classes the game as parody of its own genre. Quote: "Rare took a shot at parodying the hitherto colorful, family friendly genre". https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/funny-games-a-definition-for-and-study-of-comic-videogames
- **READ** Rare's earlier humour came from the room: the Banjo team's pranks "bled into the style of humour of the final product", and "Most of the dialogue was ad-libbed". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banjo-Kazooie_(video_game)
- **SEARCH SUMMARY** (an interview excerpt surfaced by search; page not read, source site uncertain) Seavor is quoted as saying that with a script the result "comes out really wooden, really stilted". Listed sources included https://www.scousegamer88.com/2021/01/24/the-twelve-tales-of-chris-seavor-part-iii-conkers-bad-fur-day/
- **SEARCH SUMMARY** (Splice Today, Marlow; not read) The team looked forward to each new batch of cutscene audio and would listen and "roll around laughing" (the summary's quotation). https://www.splicetoday.com/digital/conker-s-bad-fur-day-programmer-discusses-cult-classic
- **SEARCH SUMMARY** (WatchMojo; not read) Nearly the whole story is said to have been built from dialogue improvised between Seavor and Beanland. https://www.watchmojo.com/articles/10-things-you-didn-t-know-about-conkers-bad-fur-day

### Inferences (mine)
- The order of work is the lesson: voice, then staging and animation to the voice, then the gameplay that leads up to it. The joke's rhythm is fixed first and everything else is fitted to it.
- The team tested jokes on themselves. Audio that made the room laugh went in. That is a cheap and honest test for a small team.
- The humour is mostly watched, not played. The pads are the bridge: one press starts a bit whose timing the game then owns.
- Parody gave non-writers a brief. "Do the Matrix lobby" tells the artist, the composer and the programmer what to make.

### Gaps
- No first-hand statement on comic timing in animation (holds, anticipation, how long a beat was) could be read; the commentary where this is most likely discussed is video.
- How facial poses were matched to recorded lines: not found.
- Whether animators worked from video reference of the voice sessions: not found.

## Ideas this suggests for RatQuest
All of these are my suggestions, each tied to a finding above.

- **Find the Club's "beehive with guns".** One scene that made the bosses laugh became the rule for the whole game. Build one Club vignette to a finished, funny state (say, two regulars arguing at a table with a pay-off when the knight walks up) and use it as the bar every other vignette must reach.
- **Give one person the final say on tone.** The cancelled version had ideas and no direction; the shipped one had a lead who judged every scene. For a tiny team this is free and matters more than any tool.
- **Voice or mumble first, then animate.** Fix the rhythm of each exchange before posing anyone. Even with gibberish voices, lay the audio and the speech-bubble timing first.
- **Put the jokes where the pace is yours.** Humour lands when "the player can't control the pace". In the Club that means short, locked moments: a two-second reaction when you approach a table, a pay-off after an errand. Keep them short so they never feel like the two hours of cutscene Conker needed.
- **Choose props for their sound.** The frying pan was picked because it clangs. A rat knight's shield hitting a tankard, a bar bell, a spittoon: pick Club props that make a funny noise when struck, since the player will hit everything.
- **Budget for small reactions, not long scenes.** The team prioritised details like imps reacting to a typed swear word. At roughly nine seconds of animation a day by professionals, a solo developer should prefer twenty one-second reactions over one twenty-second scene.
- **Write around the people you have.** The opera boss exists because a programmer could sing. Whoever on the project can do a voice, play an instrument or draw a certain thing well should have a Club regular built for it.
- **Use parody as a brief, sparingly.** A film reference gave Conker's whole team a shared target for music, staging and play. One or two clear homages in RatQuest's worlds can do the same, without the game depending on them.
- **Build one fast tool for staging.** A language that turned three-minute changes into seconds, and a cutscene editor, are what let the team improvise. For the Club, a simple data file per vignette (who, where, which loop, which reaction line) that reloads without a rebuild would pay for itself.
- **Avoid their editor's flaw.** Their scenes had to be replayed whole to check a change. Make sure a single regular's loop or line can be previewed alone.
- **Expect to cut a fifth.** Conker shipped with about 20% of its planned play cut and almost all its writing intact. Plan Club regulars in tiers so the last tier can be dropped without holes.
- **Leave a joke where a cut was.** The barred hole with unreachable chocolate was kept on purpose. A locked back-room door in the Club that a regular refuses to explain turns unfinished space into character.
- **Be different on purpose, and know the cost.** The game was saved by not being another cute platformer, and it sold poorly partly because its tone narrowed its audience and its platform holder backed away. RatQuest's sewer-bar tone is a strength; keep the crudeness to a level that does not shut out the players its movement and combat would otherwise win.
- **Do not chase the remake's lesson backwards.** The better-looking remake lost some of the facial life and the uncensored edge, and Rare itself reissued the original. Polish that removes character is a loss; keep the rough PS1 expressiveness.
